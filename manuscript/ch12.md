## Chapter Twelve

For a while it was good, or good enough, which at twelve and then thirteen years old, with two years of ditches and barns and one very dry cornfield behind him, Wendell was willing to call the same thing.

He ate three times a day. He slept under canvas that didn't leak, in a cot that was his own and nobody else's, and he had, for the first time since his own mother's kitchen, a place he was expected to be in the morning, which turned out to be worth more to him than he'd have guessed: the plain animal comfort of being looked for. He put by money too, a little each week, folded into a sock he kept inside his boot, because some old instinct in him, maybe Munro's, maybe Vesta Coombs', maybe just his own, told him a boy in his position ought never trust a good stretch of road to keep being good.

The town crowds blurred together after a while, same as anything does when you do it every night: a church-sized tent, a banner with his name turned to advertisement, the hush, the telling, the money moving fast across a folding table after. He got better at reading a crowd, could feel in the first minute of a telling whether a town wanted comfort or spectacle or a reason to believe in something bigger than its own hard year, and could give them whichever one paid best without much more effort than turning a dial.

It was Crane, of course, who kept turning that dial further than Wendell had agreed to.

It started small, same as most erosions do. Crane suggested, one night before a telling in a town with a particularly hard reputation for tight collection plates, that Wendell might mention how he'd felt, standing in that church, a kind of presence gathering around him "like a man arriving to stand beside me, though I couldn't have said who." It was a lie, plainly. Wendell had felt nothing of the kind and had been entirely alone with a shepherd in a stained-glass window. But Crane framed it not as a lie so much as a deepening, a way of letting the crowd feel that the ministry itself, Crane's whole traveling business of it, had somehow been present at the very beginning, blessing what came after before it had properly happened.

Wendell said it that night. He heard the words come out of his own mouth in his own flat practical voice, the voice that had always meant this is true, and felt the crowd take the lie same as they took everything else he told them, without question, because the voice didn't change to warn them a falsehood was riding inside it.

He hated how easy it was. That was the thing that stayed with him longest, longer than the money did, longer than the shame. Not that he'd lied, exactly, since a boy on the road learns early that a small lie is sometimes the cheapest toll a situation charges, but that the lie had cost him nothing to tell. No catch in his throat. No hitch in the flat voice. It had gone out into the tent smooth as anything true, and the crowd had received it exactly the same. Walking back to his cot that night with more money in his sock than he'd ever carried at once, Wendell understood that the gift Munro had named for him at that thin little road-fire, a strangeness folks couldn't place, one that could be used instead of merely endured, cut in a direction Munro hadn't warned him about either. It could be used on people same as it had once been used against him, and the ease of that, the sheer lack of friction in it, frightened him worse than any lantern-lit shotgun or drought-country mob ever had, because none of those had asked him to become the thing doing the frightening.

He told himself it was one line, one night, one town he'd likely never pass through again. He told himself that the following week, and the week after. Each time it was one more small thing Crane asked him to add or sand away: a detail about the water's spring, a suggestion that his prayer had somehow known, in advance, that a man like Crane existed to carry the story further. Each time, Wendell found the flat voice willing to carry it, a little further each week, a boot walking a little further into water before a man notices he's stopped being able to see the bottom he started on.
