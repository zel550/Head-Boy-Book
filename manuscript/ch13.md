## Chapter Thirteen

The ask, when it came, came dressed as a small thing, the same way all the others had, and Wendell would think for a long time afterward about how much of Crane's whole trade was built on making the large things arrive wearing the clothes of the small ones.

It was a town near the state line, bigger than most they'd played, with three churches and a bank and a general store that sold bottled soda, and Crane had been eyeing it for two days the way a man eyes a field he means to get everything out of before he moves on. The night before the telling, he sat Wendell down by the cashbox, same as that first night, and said, easy as anything, "I've been thinking on how we open tomorrow. I want to try something new on this bigger crowd. I want you to say your mother sent away for a bottle of the water beforehand — sprinkled it on you the morning of, is how I'm thinking we tell it — and that's what carried you down that road to the church in the first place. Ties the whole thing together nice and neat. Gives the water its due at the start instead of just the end."

Wendell sat with that a moment, the way he'd learned to sit with things before answering, and understood, plain and total, what was actually being asked of him, underneath the easy delivery. Not a detail added. Not a corner sanded. He was being asked to take the one true, unbent, unbought thing left in the whole telling — his mother at the sink with her hands in dishwater, saying *then you ought to go ask Him yourself* out of her own worn-down honesty, owing nothing to anybody, wanting nothing sold off the back of it — and paint Crane's water into the very center of it, retroactively, as though the truest and most private thing that had ever happened between him and his mother had been Crane's doing all along, dressed in a bottle Crane sold for two bits.

"No," Wendell said.

Crane didn't so much as blink. "It's a small change, son. Nobody in that crowd tomorrow was standing at your mother's sink. Nobody's going to march up here and correct the record."

"I'd know," Wendell said. "That's the whole of it. I'd know, standing up there, that I'd sold the one thing that wasn't mine to sell. You can have the rest. You've had the rest for a year near enough, and I let you have it because I told myself it was small, one line at a time, and I was wrong about that, I see it now, but this isn't small, and I said at the start she stayed out of it, and I meant that the way I've never meant near enough else I've said up on that platform."

Crane looked at him a long moment, the arithmetic behind his stillness working harder than Wendell had ever seen it work, and when he spoke again his voice had lost the easy warmth it usually carried, gone instead to something flatter, more like the sound underneath the sound, the machinery with its cover off.

"You understand," Crane said, "that a testimony's only worth what it moves in a crowd, and a boy who kept every word of it exactly true from the first telling to the last would've starved on that road two winters back. You didn't come to me pure, son. You came to me hungry, same as I found you, and you've eaten fine off every dressed-up word since, and it strikes me as a strange place to plant a flag, over one line about a woman who isn't even here to know it was said."

"She'd know," Wendell said. "I'd tell her. I've never once lied to her in my life and I'm not fixing to start now, over a bottle of water, to a crowd in a town I'll never see again."

"Then you understand," Crane said, "that we've come to the end of what you and I can do business over," and there was nothing cruel in how he said it, which was somehow worse than if there had been, because it meant Crane had already, in the space of that short conversation, finished his own arithmetic and moved clean past Wendell to whatever came next, the way a man closes a ledger on an account that's stopped paying.

Wendell went back to his cot that night and did not sleep much, turning over the size of what he'd just done — refused a year's worth of full plates and dry cots and a place he was expected to be each morning, over a single sentence about his mother that no one in that town would ever have known was false — and found, turning it over, that he didn't regret it, not even a little, which surprised him more than the asking had.
