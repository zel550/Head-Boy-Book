# WENDELL ROLLS

*(working title: THE HEAD)*

## Chapter One

The boy was born a head. Nothing else. His mother pushed for eleven hours and out came something the size of a cantaloupe with a face on it, and that was the whole baby. No arms. No legs. No body to speak of, just a stump of neck that sealed itself up neat as a scar, like the rest of him had never been ordered in the first place.

The doctor didn't say anything for a while. Then he said, "Well," and that was all the medical opinion anybody got that day.

They named him Wendell, after her father, because a name was one thing they could still give him in full.

He grew the way heads grow, which is to say slowly and without much to show for it. He learned to talk early because talking was the only thing available to him. He learned to blink meaningfully. He learned to roll — first as an accident, then as a method, then as a kind of dignity, the same way a man learns to make peace with a limp. His mother would find him in the mornings out in the yard, having rolled himself clean off the porch in the night, lying in the grass looking up at the sky like he'd meant to be there.

His father did not roll him anywhere. His father worked at the feed mill and came home smelling like it and sat in the chair that faced away from the kitchen, and for the first six years he mostly managed not to look at Wendell directly, the way you don't look directly at the sun or at a car wreck, because some part of you understands that looking makes it real in a way that not-looking doesn't.

"He's got your eyes," people used to say to her, at the start, before people stopped saying much of anything.

Wendell didn't think of himself as unfinished. That was a thing that came from outside him, the way weather comes from outside a house. He thought of himself, when he thought of himself at all, as a boy who got around a little different than other boys, the way some boys had red hair and some boys stuttered and he happened to be the kind of boy who traveled by rolling and ate what was put directly in front of his mouth and never once in his life had scraped a knee, on account of not having knees to scrape, which he considered, on balance, a point in his favor.

It was his mother who said the thing about God.

He was seven, or near enough — nobody was keeping close count, since birthdays for a head are a private sort of sadness nobody wants to throw a party for — and he'd said to her, plain as asking for a glass of milk, "I want arms and legs."

She was up to her elbows in dishwater and she didn't turn around right away. When she did, she looked at him a long moment, the way you look at a bill you can't pay, and she said, "Then you ought to go on and ask Him yourself. I've asked enough for the both of us and He hasn't seen fit to answer me. Maybe He'll answer a boy."

She meant it, near as he could tell, the way tired people mean the last true thing they've got left to say.

So the next morning, before the heat came up, Wendell rolled himself down the porch steps — one, two, three, a small controlled violence he'd long since stopped flinching at — and out along the dirt road toward the church, which sat at the end of a mile and a half of gravel like a tooth somebody had forgotten to pull.

It took him most of the morning. Rolling is not a fast way to travel, and it is a humbling one; twice he rolled into the ditch and lay there a while, watching clouds, before working himself back up onto the crown of the road. A dog followed him part of the way and then lost interest, the way dogs do with things they can't chase properly. By the time the church came into view he was dust-colored all over, the same dust-colored as the road, so that a person driving by might've mistaken him for something that had fallen off a truck.

The church was empty on a Tuesday, which he was glad of. He did not want an audience for what he understood, even then, to be either a miracle or an embarrassment, and possibly both at once, and he did not know which would be worse to have witnessed.

He rolled himself up the aisle — a long haul, that aisle, longer than it looked from the door — and came to rest below the front, in a bar of colored light coming through the one good window, the one with the shepherd in it.

He didn't know the right words. Nobody had taught him any, on account of nobody quite knowing what to teach a boy in his situation. So he just said what was true.

"I'd like arms and legs, if that's all right. I don't need them to be anything special. I'll take whatever's around."

Then he waited.

Nothing happened for a long time, long enough that he started to feel foolish, lying there in the colored light talking to the dust motes, and he'd about decided to roll himself back home and not mention it to anybody, when he felt a kind of pulling low down where his neck sealed up, not painful exactly, more like a limb waking up after sitting on it wrong, and then there was a sound like wet rope, and then he had a body.

It came out of him the way a plant comes out of the ground, if the ground were flesh-colored and the plant were a fully formed human boy — arms first, then a trunk, then legs, all of it pale and slick and new, unfolding itself out of the base of his neck like it had been packed in there all along, waiting on permission.

He sat up. He had never sat up before. It was the strangest thing he'd ever done.

He looked at his hands — five fingers each, working fine, a little damp — and he started to laugh, and the laughing turned into something closer to crying, and he let it, there on the floor of the church, a naked boy with a full set of limbs and nobody there to see it but the shepherd in the window, who kept right on holding his glass lamb and didn't say a word about it either way.

Wendell walked home. It took him twenty minutes what had taken all morning by roll, and he spent most of it falling down, because walking turns out to be a skill and not a gift, whatever else he'd been given for free that day. He fell in the ditch. He fell on the gravel and got gravel in his new palms and it hurt in a way that lying in a ditch as a head had never hurt, and he understood dimly that this was going to be a running theme.

His mother was at the clothesline when she saw him coming up the road on two legs, and she dropped the whole basket in the dirt.

She didn't ask him how. She held him and cried the way people cry when a long debt finally comes due, not gladness exactly, more like relief with the wind knocked out of it, and she got him into some of his uncle's old clothes, which hung off him like a tent, and she fixed him a plate, and for the first time in his life he fed himself, badly, elbows everywhere, and she watched him do it like it was the finest thing she'd ever seen a person do.

His father came home at six, same as always, smelling like the mill, same as always, and stopped in the door with his hand still on the frame.

He looked at the boy sitting at his table. Arms. Legs. A whole boy, sitting there eating a plate of beans with his hands, laughing every so often at nothing in particular, the way a person laughs when the world has just proven itself stranger than they'd allowed for.

His father did not say anything for a long moment. Then he set his lunch pail down slow, like it might go off, and he said, "No."

That was all. Just the one word.

His mother started to explain — the church, the prayer, the way the boy had come rolling up the road on legs he'd never owned — but his father wasn't listening anymore. He was looking at Wendell the way you look at a thing that has broken a rule you didn't know was a rule until it broke, and something in his face had gone shut, like a door pulled to in a wind.

"That is not a thing that happens," his father said. "That is not a thing that is allowed to happen in a house I live in."

"He prayed," his mother said. "You told him to."

"I did not tell him to grow himself a body out of the air."

"He didn't grow it out of the air, he grew it out of —"

"I don't care where he grew it out of." His father's voice had gone very quiet, which was worse than loud. "I have spent six years learning how to sit in a room with a head. I built that. I built a whole way of being a father to a boy with no arms and no legs, and I got good at it, God forgive me, I got good at loving a thing I couldn't hardly look at. And now it's up and it's got hands and it's eating beans at my table like nothing happened, like I didn't spend six years —"

He stopped. He looked like a man who'd heard himself and didn't care for what he'd heard, but wasn't going to take it back either.

"I can't," he said. "I can't do this one too. I did the head. I can't do the boy."

He picked his pail back up. He didn't take anything else — no bag, no goodbye, nothing you could call a leaving with any ceremony to it — he just walked back out the door he'd walked in through ninety seconds before, and got in the truck, and drove off in the direction of the mill even though the mill was closed for the night, because a man needs somewhere to drive to when he's leaving and the truth is he's got nowhere at all.

He did not come back that night. He did not come back the next one either.

Wendell sat at the table with his plate of beans going cold and his new hands folded in his lap, and he looked at his mother, and he said, in the flat and practical voice he'd use for the rest of his life to say the worst things that happened to him, "I think that's my fault."

His mother didn't tell him it wasn't. She'd never been a woman for comfortable lies. She just pulled a chair up next to him and said, "You're going to need shoes. Whatever else is true, you're going to need shoes," and that was as close to a plan as either of them had, and it would have to do.
