## Chapter Six

The town was called Loman's Fork, and Wendell arrived in it same as he arrived in most towns by then: on foot, alone, with nothing on him a person could look at and call a plan.

The collection plate went missing from the Baptist church on a Wednesday, sometime between the evening service and the morning somebody noticed, and Wendell had been seen, by two separate people who agreed on very little else, sleeping under the eave of the feed store not two hundred yards from the church steps that same night.

Nobody asked him first. That was the part he noted, and kept noting. Nobody walked up and said, boy, did you take the church's money. What happened instead was that a deputy came and found him at the feed store eave the next morning and said, "You'll want to come with me," in the flat voice men use when they've already decided a thing and are only going through the motions of the asking.

The jail in Loman's Fork was one room with two cells in it, and Wendell sat in the one that wasn't holding a drunk from the night before, and he waited, because waiting was a thing he'd gotten good at, same as he'd once gotten good at rolling, not glad of it, just competent.

What happened next took him longer to understand than it should have, and he thought about it for years afterward, turning it in his hands like a coin he wasn't sure was real.

Word got around fast, same as it always did in a town with one gas station and one church and no good reason not to talk, about who exactly was sitting in that cell. Not a name. Nobody in Loman's Fork knew his name. What got around was a description, and the description picked up weight town to town, porch to porch, until the boy sleeping under the feed store eave had become, by suppertime, something closer to an occurrence than a person: the boy from the story, the one they'd heard about two counties over, the one who wasn't right, who'd done something to himself or had something done to him that a body wasn't meant to do or have done.

The sheriff, when he finally came by near dusk, didn't look at Wendell the deputy's way. He had the look of a man who'd found a snake under the porch and didn't want to get close enough to be sure what kind it was, wanting mainly for it to be somewhere else.

"You take that plate?" the sheriff asked, from a good six feet back, farther than a man needs to stand from a hungry twelve-year-old to ask him a simple question.

"No, sir," Wendell said.

The sheriff looked at him a while longer. Wendell watched him not want to come any closer, not want to sit across a table and go at the thing properly, pressing, watching his face, catching him in the small lies boys tell out of simple fright even when they're innocent of the big one. None of that happened. The sheriff stood his six feet back and made his decision out of something that had very little to do with the plate at all.

"I want you gone from this county by morning," he said. "And I don't want to hear another word about you in it, ever again, for any reason, good or bad. You understand me?"

"Yes, sir," Wendell said, and did not say the other thing, which was that he understood something more particular than that, something he'd go on turning over long after Loman's Fork was a smudge behind him. He was not being let go because anyone believed him. He was being let go because the closeness a real accusation requires, the room they'd have needed to sit in to properly investigate him, was a room nobody in that jailhouse wanted to be in with him. His innocence hadn't won out. Their fear had simply outweighed their certainty, and the two had settled the matter between themselves without much reference to him at all.

He walked out of Loman's Fork before light, same as he'd walked out of his own house, and no one came to see him off, and no one ever did find out who took the church's money. Wendell carried both of those facts down the road with him, the not-being-punished and the not-being-cleared, and understood it in the slow manner a person comes to understand a bruise, less by the moment it was made than by the color it turns after: that a reputation which keeps you safe and a reputation which keeps you guilty can be, from the inside of it, the very same reputation, worn at the very same time. There wasn't a thing he could do about which one a given town decided to use on him. Only about how steady he stood while they decided.
