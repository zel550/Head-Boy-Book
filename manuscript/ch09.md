## Chapter Nine

By the time he reached a town big enough to have a proper square with a bandstand in it, Wendell had been on the road the better part of two years, and he'd learned enough about the shape of his own reputation, how it cut him and how it covered him in near enough equal measure, that he no longer expected either outcome walking into a new place. He mostly just watched, quiet, to see which way the coin was going to land.

There was a tent up in the square when he came through, bigger than any tent had a right to be for a town that size, with a banner strung across the front in red letters that had clearly been repainted more than once, each time a little more urgent than the last: BROTHER TOBIAS CRANE, HEALER OF THE AFFLICTED, WITNESS AND BE MADE WHOLE.

Wendell stood at the back of the crowd, close enough to see and far enough to leave quick, same as he'd learned to stand at the back of most crowds, and he watched a man work.

Crane was not a large man, but he moved like one, all gesture and pause, some men just knowing how to fill up more of a room than their body technically occupies, and a voice pitched to reach the back row without seeming to try. He had a woman up on the platform who couldn't walk without a cane, and by the time he'd laid his hands on her and talked over her for the better part of ten minutes, about faith, about the county's hard year, about a God who rewarded belief with visible proof of itself, she was walking a slow half-circle around the platform without the cane at all, to a sound from the crowd that Wendell recognized immediately. He'd heard something close to it once from a church pew he wasn't in, on a Tuesday that felt now like it belonged to somebody else's life.

He watched the whole show. He watched Crane sell bottles of a water he swore had been prayed over at a spring that healed what doctors couldn't, two bits a bottle, three for a half dollar. He watched the woman with the cane collect hers for free and disappear into the crowd afterward, and though he didn't see it, he suspected she'd be back the next night in the next town Crane's tent went up in, walking the same slow healed half-circle for a different set of faces.

What struck Wendell, standing at the back of that crowd with his hands in his pockets, wasn't the fraud of it, though he understood the fraud of it plainly enough. What struck him was how familiar the whole machine was to him, gear for gear. A town needed a wonder to believe in or a danger to blame, and it would find one whether or not the boy or the woman in the middle of it had done a single thing to earn either role. He had been the wonder, once, in a church his mother had sent him to on a dare. He had been the danger, more times than he cared to count, in cornfields and jailhouses that never once bothered to ask him a real question before deciding what he was. And here was a man who had found a way to stand at the center of that same machine on purpose, running the crank himself instead of being fed through it, and walking away every night with money in his pocket for the trouble.

Wendell thought about Munro, gone two years now without so much as a goodbye, saying a strangeness folks can't name is worth more than you think, if you quit waiting on somebody else to spend it for you. He thought about Vesta Coombs boiling water without asking a single question that wasn't hers to ask. He thought about the sheriff at Loman's Fork, standing six feet back from a boy he didn't have the stomach to properly accuse. All of it had been the same machine, working on him whether he willed it or not. In two years of walking, he had never once tried steering it himself.

He waited until the crowd had thinned and the lanterns around the platform were being doused one by one, and he walked up to the man counting a cashbox by the light of the last one, and he said, in the same flat, practical voice he used for the things that mattered most to him, "I heard what you told that woman about being made whole. I know a story about that same subject you haven't heard, and I expect it'd fill your tent better than a spring you never visited."

Tobias Crane looked up at him slow, an old hand recognizing the particular sound of an opportunity walking toward him on its own two legs. He set the cashbox down, and he smiled the smile Wendell would come to know very well in the months ahead, warm enough to be believed and careful enough to cost him nothing.

"Son," Crane said, "why don't you sit down, and tell me everything, from the beginning, and let's you and I discuss what a story like that might be worth to the both of us."
