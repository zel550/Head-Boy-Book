## Chapter Fourteen

Crane put him up on the platform anyway.

Wendell hadn't expected it, not after the conversation by the cashbox, but Crane had a tent already promised to a paying crowd and a banner already strung, and a man in Crane's line of work did not, Wendell was learning even in the last hour he'd ever work for him, let a disagreement over principle get in the way of a night's collection if there was any way around it.

What Crane did instead was worse than firing him outright, and cleverer, and Wendell understood the cleverness of it even as it was happening to him. Crane took the introduction himself, same as always, working the crowd down to its hush, and then, before Wendell had said a single word, Crane told it: the version with the bottle in it, the mother sprinkling water she'd sent away for, the whole tidy lie stitched into the story's very beginning, told to several hundred people as plain fact. Then he turned and gestured Wendell up onto the platform to, as he put it, "bear witness to it himself."

Wendell understood the gamble the moment he felt the crowd's eyes land on him. Crane was betting that a boy who'd said yes to a hundred small dressings-up over a year would say yes to one more, especially with the lie already spoken and hanging in the air before he'd so much as opened his mouth. The easiest thing, the thing his own smooth untroubled voice would want to do, was simply not contradict a story the crowd had already half-swallowed.

He stood there a moment with all those faces turned up at him, the same hush he'd learned to love the sound of, and he thought about his mother at the sink with her hands in dishwater, and about the empty church on a Tuesday, and about a stone he'd been carrying in his own pocket for a year now, the one piece of the story he'd sanded off himself without being asked: the ending where his father set down a lunch pail and drove off toward a mill that wasn't open.

"That's not how it happened," Wendell said, into the hush, and felt the whole tent shift under him like a floor taking weight it hadn't expected.

He told it then, the whole of it, standing up there with Crane gone rigid a few feet off and no longer smiling anything at all. He told about the roll down the porch steps and the dust-colored road and the words he'd actually said, which hadn't needed any bottle to carry them, because there hadn't been a bottle, there'd only been a boy and an empty church and a shepherd in a window who didn't say a word about it either way. And then, because he was already standing in the truth of it and found he didn't want to climb back out, he told the rest too, the part he'd sanded off a year ago for the sake of a tidier ending: that his father had come home, and looked at a boy with a whole body sitting at his table, and said one word, and then had driven off into a night he never fully came back from, because there are people who cannot love a thing once it stops needing to be carried.

The crowd didn't cheer for that part. Some of them looked uncomfortable, the look people get when a thing they came to be uplifted by turns out to have a father in it who couldn't manage love, and Wendell watched the collection buckets, off to the side, slow down and near enough stop. He had just cost Tobias Crane a great deal of money, on purpose, in front of several hundred witnesses, with nothing to show for it himself but the plain fact of having finally said a true thing all the way to its true end.

Crane found him behind the tent afterward, in the dark between wagons, and there was no showman's warmth left in him at all, just the flat sound underneath the sound that Wendell had heard once already by the cashbox.

"You've cost me a night's take and likely a week's worth of towns down the road from here, once word travels," Crane said. "I fed you a year. I put a roof over you. And you stood up there and gutted my whole operation over one sentence about a woman who'll never know the difference."

"She'd have known," Wendell said. "That was the whole of it."

"You'll regret this," Crane said, quiet, nothing performed in it now, no crowd to work, just a man doing his own private arithmetic on what a debt like this one might eventually be worth collecting on. "A boy who costs me money is a boy I remember the name of. You go on and remember I said so."

Wendell walked out of that county before light, same as he'd walked out of Loman's Fork and his own father's house before it, with less money in his sock than he'd carried in a long while and a warning at his back he knew, even then, wasn't an idle one. But he walked lighter than he had in a year of full plates and dry cots. Sitting with it on the road, same as he sat with everything, he saw why. For the first time since a church on a Tuesday, he had told his own story, whole and unbent, and nobody had gotten a cut of it but him.
