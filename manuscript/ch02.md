## Chapter Two

Word got around fast, in a place with one gas station and one church and no good reason not to talk. By Sunday half the county had heard that the Pruitt boy (the head, they called him, not unkindly, just efficiently, same as you'd call a one-eyed dog Patch) had gone and grown himself a body at the altar rail like a garden coming up overnight.

Some people believed it outright and called it a miracle. Those people brought casseroles. Some people didn't believe a word of it and figured the family had been hiding a whole boy in a closet for six years for reasons of their own, insurance money most likely, and those people didn't bring anything, though they did slow their cars down going past the house. And some people believed it and didn't care for it at all, on the grounds that a miracle was supposed to happen to somebody who deserved one, and what had the Pruitts ever done to deserve two boys for the price of one raising.

Wendell didn't understand yet that a miracle needs an audience it can trust, and that a town is not that. He was too busy learning to be a body.

Nobody tells you that walking is work. His mother taught him like she'd teach a foal: mostly by staying out of it and letting him fall down enough times that his legs figured out the arrangement on their own. He fell off the porch the same spot he used to roll off it. He fell into the woodstove and burned the back of one new hand and cried about it for an hour, not from the pain exactly but from the insult of it. A head doesn't burn itself on a stove. A head just sits there being a head, and now here he was, vulnerable in a hundred directions he'd never had to think about.

"Everything's got an edge now," he told his mother, holding his bandaged hand like it belonged to somebody else. "I didn't used to have edges."

"Everybody's got edges," she said. "You're just finding out about yours late."

He learned to eat with a fork, mostly. He learned that legs get restless if you don't use them and that this restlessness has no name and no cure but standing up. He learned that a body needs looking after in about nine hundred small ways a head never had: teeth, and nails, and the business of relieving yourself, which he found humiliating in a way he never had as a head. A head has some dignity in its limitations. A body is just one long negotiation with your own indignity, forever.

But he also learned things he liked. He learned that hands were good for holding a cat, and that a cat held correctly will fall asleep on you like you're furniture it trusts. He learned that legs, once they got the hang of themselves, could run, and that running was the finest thing anybody had ever kept secret from him his whole life, six years wasted not knowing that a person could just decide to go fast and then be fast. He ran the whole back pasture the first time he really got the trick of it and came back with grass stains up both new knees and a look on his face his mother said she'd remember on her deathbed. It was the first time she'd ever seen him look, simply, like a boy having a good day, with none of the old carefulness in it.

His father did not come home in three days, and then he did, and didn't say anything about the boy at the table, just ate his supper looking at his plate and went to bed early. This became the pattern. He was a man performing the shape of living in his own house without ever quite agreeing to be in it. He'd nod at Wendell in the hall like he'd nod at a boarder he didn't much care for but wasn't in a position to evict. He never once, in all the months that followed, touched him. Not a hand on the shoulder, not a ruffle of the hair, nothing. Wendell had spent six years being carried and lifted and handled with a kind of ferocious tenderness, on account of having no other way to move through the world, and he felt the absence of that touch more sharply than he'd ever felt its presence.

"He carried me everywhere," Wendell said to his mother one night, not sad about it exactly, just working out a fact. "Before. He used to carry me down to the creek on Sundays. Now I can walk down there myself and he won't even look at me going."

"He misses the head," she said. It was the closest she ever came to saying something cruel about his father, and she said it like a diagnosis, not an accusation. "He learned to love the thing he could carry. He hasn't figured out yet how to love the thing that can walk off on its own."

Wendell sat with that a long while, the size of it too big to use right away but too important to throw out, the kind of sentence a mother hands a boy without warning and expects him to carry on his own from then on. He decided, in the end, that this was a stupid reason to leave a boy sitting alone at supper every night. That decision, quiet and unspectacular, made somewhere around age eight while chewing on a piece of cornbread, was the first hairline crack in whatever had held that house together.
