## Chapter Fifteen

He was fourteen by the time he came into the mill town, poorer than he'd been in a year, on account of a debt he now carried that had nothing to do with money. A warning spoken behind a tent in the dark had him checking over his shoulder some evenings, without meaning to, and he was trying, with more effort than skill, to lift a loaf of bread off a bakery windowsill where it had been set out to cool, when a voice behind him said, "You're bad at that."

He turned around holding the bread already, which settled the question of whether he'd deny it. The girl leaning in the alley mouth looked to be near his own age, maybe a year older, with a wooden leg below one knee that she wore plain, the way another girl might wear a bad haircut, present, unhidden, and entirely beside the point as far as she seemed concerned.

"Bad how," Wendell said, since there wasn't much use pretending otherwise.

"You looked both ways down the street four times before you touched it. A person that nervous might as well carry a sign." She pushed off the wall and came toward him, and Wendell noted, same as he noted most things, that she didn't favor the wooden leg or hide it under her skirt hem, just walked on it plain as anything, a little rolling gait that had clearly stopped costing her any particular thought a good while back. "Mrs. Ordway'll have you in front of the sheriff over one loaf if she catches you. She's put boys away for less out of pure boredom."

"Then I'd best eat it quick," Wendell said, "before evidence."

That got something out of her, not quite a laugh, more the short exhale of a person deciding a stranger has earned one more sentence of her time. "Come on, then, before she does catch you, and I'll feed you something that isn't stolen, on account of I can't watch a job done that poorly without wanting to fix it."

Her name was Ruth Calloway, and she'd lost the leg two years back to the carding machine at the mill where near everyone in that town worked sooner or later, a fact she delivered to him within the first ten minutes of knowing him, plainly, without a single beat of the pause people usually put before a thing like that, same as she'd mention what county she was born in.

"You're not going to ask me the question," she said, watching him not ask it, over a plate of bread and butter in the back room of the boardinghouse where she rented and did the mending for her keep.

"What question."

"The one everybody asks eventually. How'd it happen, does it hurt now, do I miss it." She said all three in the flat singsong of a person reciting something worn smooth by overuse. "You've got the look of a boy who's had worse things asked of him than that, so I figured you might skip it."

"I wasn't going to ask," Wendell said, "on account of I've had the leg end of that question aimed at me plenty, just about a different set of limbs, and I never did care for the answers I gave, so I don't see much use asking somebody else to give me one either."

She looked at him a beat longer than the conversation strictly required, some new arithmetic working behind her face, the same kind he'd watched work behind Silas Boyd's face and the sheriff at Loman's Fork's and Crane's, except hers didn't seem to be deciding what he was so much as deciding whether he was worth the trouble of finding out.

"Word does travel," she said, "even to a mill town. The boy who grew himself whole." She said the banner-line without a trace of the reverence it usually got, more like repeating a joke she'd heard secondhand and wasn't sure landed. "You don't look like much of a miracle sitting there with butter on your chin."

"I'm not," Wendell said. "I'm a boy who got lucky once and unlucky a great deal more since, same as anybody."

"Well," Ruth said, plain and unimpressed, and something in how she said it put him for a moment right back on the floor of that church, hearing the same word out of a different mouth entirely, "that's the first true thing anybody's told me all week, and I'll take it over a miracle any day. Eat your bread. Mrs. Ordway keeps a schedule and I've got mending to finish before she starts asking after me instead of you."
