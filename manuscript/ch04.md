## Chapter Four

The first night he slept in a barn belonging to a man named Silas Boyd, though Wendell didn't know that yet, didn't know anything about the place except that it had a roof and the roof didn't leak and there was hay enough to make a shape in it that a boy could disappear into if he tried.

He'd walked most of the day without much plan beyond putting distance between himself and the last thing that had been said at his own supper table. Walking, it turned out, was a fine occupation for a boy with nothing to think about except walking. By the time the light went he'd covered more ground than he'd ever covered in his life, on legs he'd had for all of five years, and his feet had raised two blisters that would each go on to have long and difficult careers of their own.

He climbed into the loft the way a raccoon climbs into an attic, which is to say quietly and with the specific guilt of something that knows it hasn't been invited, and he burrowed down and was asleep before he'd finished deciding whether he should be.

He woke to a lantern in his face and a shotgun a little lower than the lantern, which he understood, even half-asleep, to be the more important of the two.

"Don't move," the man holding both of them said, which was unnecessary advice, since Wendell had gone the particular kind of still that a body goes when it has decided, on its own and without consulting the person attached to it, that stillness is the whole of the plan.

The man was somewhere past fifty, built like a fence post, with the specific tiredness of a man who has just been woken up to discover the thing he feared finding in his barn is worse, in its way, than the thing he'd imagined. He held the lantern up and looked at Wendell's face a long time, and something happened in his own face that Wendell had gotten used to seeing on people, a kind of arithmetic that didn't come out even — a boy's face, but a boy's face on a boy who didn't look quite finished being decided on, the way a sentence looks before somebody's put the last word on it.

"What are you," the man said. Not who. Wendell noted that, the way he noted most things now, filing it someplace and not commenting on it, because commenting on it never seemed to help.

"A boy," Wendell said. "Just sleeping in your barn. I'll go on."

"You will," the man said, "go on. But not in the dark, because a boy that walks off into my dark after I've caught him in my barn is a boy I'll have to explain to somebody eventually, one way or another, and I'd as soon not."

He didn't lower the gun so much as forget about it, the way a man forgets he's still holding a hammer after the nail's gone in. He looked at Wendell another while, and Wendell understood he was being weighed against every story that had ever traveled through that county about strange things in barns, changelings and vagrants and boys who weren't right, and that whatever tipped the scale wasn't going to be anything Wendell said, because there wasn't a sentence built that could out-argue a lantern at midnight.

What tipped it, in the end, was smaller than that. Wendell's stomach made a sound. An ordinary, undignified, hungry-boy sound, the same sound any boy's stomach would make after a day of walking on two blistered feet with half a loaf of bread eaten by noon, and something in the man's face came unstuck when he heard it, the way suspicion will sometimes come unstuck at the exact moment a thing in front of you does something too ordinary to be dangerous.

"Come on down," the man said. "I ain't going to shoot a boy for being hungry in my barn. That'd be a hell of a thing to have to live with over a half a bag of chicken feed."

His name was Silas Boyd, though he didn't offer it and Wendell didn't ask, and Wendell only learned it later, from somebody else, in the way you learn the names of places you've only passed through once. He gave Wendell a plate of cold beans standing up in the kitchen doorway, not inviting him further in than the step, and he watched him eat the way you'd watch an animal you weren't sure yet whether to trust around the henhouse.

"You got people?" he asked, when the plate was near clean.

"Not anymore," Wendell said, which was the truest thing he had to offer and also, he understood even as he said it, not quite the whole shape of the truth, since he did have people, technically, they had simply stopped being the kind of people a boy could go back to.

Boyd didn't push on it. He had the look of a man who'd learned a long time back that some doors, once a boy's walked out of them, don't profit from being asked about.

"Be gone by light," he said. "Not because I want you gone. Because a boy nobody can account for, found in a man's barn, is a story that goes one of two ways in this county, and I've seen both, and neither one of them's got a place in it for me being the fool that fed you first and asked questions after."

He didn't say it unkindly. He said it the way you'd read a weather report to somebody about to travel — not glad of the weather, just honest about it.

Wendell was gone before the sun cleared the tree line, his stomach fuller than it had been in a day and his feet no better for the rest, and he understood something on the road that morning that he'd turn over for a long time after: that the same not-being-able-to-place-him that had put a gun in his face at midnight was the very thing that had gotten him a plate of beans an hour later, and that there wasn't any way to know, walking up on a stranger's lantern light, which one of those two things you were about to get. It wasn't a lesson with a moral in it. It was just a fact about the road, the first of many, and he filed it next to the others and kept walking.
