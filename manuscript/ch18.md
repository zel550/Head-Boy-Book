## Chapter Eighteen

He offered to leave that same week, the way he figured a decent person ought to, once a danger of his own making had been laid out plain on somebody else's kitchen table.

"You could put me out," he told Ruth, "and I wouldn't hold it against you. Man like Crane doesn't forget easy, and I've no business letting that follow me into a house that isn't mine to risk."

Ruth looked at him the particular length of time she seemed to reserve for deciding whether a thing he'd said deserved a serious answer or a short one, and gave him, in the end, a serious one.

"If I put out everybody in this town who's got a debt or a grudge trailing them somewhere behind, Wendell, I'd be renting an empty shed to nobody at all, and Mrs. Ordway'd have to find some other cousin to gossip about. Half this town's carrying something they don't talk about at supper. You're only unusual in that yours has a name and a banner and a tent attached to it." She went back to her mending, which he'd learned was her way of signaling a matter settled rather than one still open for debate. "I didn't say all that to run you off. I said it so you'd stop pretending the ground under your own feet wasn't real. That's a different thing entirely."

So he stayed, and the staying, once it had been named out loud and agreed to by both of them instead of simply happening around the edges of an unspoken arrangement, felt different to him than it had before — steadier, the way a plank feels different underfoot once you know for certain it's nailed down rather than merely resting where you left it.

He let himself, for the first time since a church on a Tuesday, imagine an ordinary future, and found the imagining came easier than he'd expected, the way a held breath comes easier once you've finally noticed you were holding it. He pictured himself grown a few years further into the mending trade, maybe with a shop of his own someday, small, front room and a back room, his name over the door in plain black letters that weren't advertising anything but a trade honestly done. He pictured suppers that weren't shared with strangers who'd forget his face by morning. He pictured, though he didn't let himself linger on it too directly, being someone Ruth Calloway might choose to keep around on purpose rather than out of the plain decency she seemed to extend to anyone hurt enough to land on her fence line, the way she'd have put it, though this time the fence line was a bakery alley and the hurt was three years old and mostly healed over into something else, something he didn't yet have the right word for.

"You've gone quiet," Ruth said, one evening, catching him at the window instead of at the road, which she noted the way she noted most things, plainly and without much ceremony made of it.

"Thinking on staying," he said. "Never let myself think on it serious before. Always figured a boy like me was built for moving, on account of moving was the only thing that ever kept me safe when staying wasn't offered."

"And now it's being offered."

"Now it's being offered," he said, "and I find I don't hardly know what to do with the offer, having never held one like it before. Feels like learning to walk all over again. Something a body's supposed to just know how to do, and doesn't, not the first hundred times it tries."

"Well," Ruth said, "you were bad at stealing bread too, the first time I saw you try it. Man that keeps at a thing badly long enough generally gets less bad at it. I don't see why staying ought to be any different a skill than the rest."

It was, Wendell thought, watching her go back to her needle in the lamp light, about as close to a plain invitation as Ruth Calloway was ever likely to hand a person outright, and he understood, sitting with it, that he meant to take her up on it, for as long as the world was willing to let him.

The world, as it turned out, was willing to let him have four more months of it before it came looking for its due.
