## Chapter Twenty-Four

He stayed a week, long enough to split a good deal of wood and mend the porch step that had never once been fixed since he used to roll off it as a boy, and long enough for his mother to say most of what three years apart had left unsaid between them, and then he told her he meant to go back south, and she didn't try to stop him, same as she never had.

"There a reason this time," she asked, "or is it just the road calling, same as it always used to call your uncle before he settled."

"There's a reason," Wendell said. "Her name's Ruth. She's got a leg she lost same as I got a body I grew, and she's spent longer than me learning how to stay somewhere on purpose, and I mean to go on back and see does she still want the company, now that the trouble that chased me here's had time to lose interest, same as she said it would."

His mother studied him a while, the same long study she'd given him the day he left at twelve, except there was none of the grief in it now that there'd been then, only something closer to satisfaction, the particular kind a woman allows herself once a debt she'd worried over a long while finally comes due in a shape she can live with.

"Well," she said, "you'd best go on, then, and not keep a patient woman waiting past her patience." She packed him a proper sack this time, more than two shirts and half a loaf of guilty bread, and she stood at the same window she'd stood at three years before, and this time when he went down the porch steps and out onto the road, she was smiling instead of grieving, which he carried with him the whole first mile like something warm held against his chest.

He walked south the same road he'd once walked away on, past the church at the end of its mile and a half of gravel, still standing, still the same tooth somebody had forgotten to pull, and he stopped there a while, the first time he'd gone inside it since he was seven years old lying in a bar of colored light asking a room full of dust motes for whatever was around.

The shepherd was still in the window, still holding his glass lamb, still not saying a word about anything either way. Wendell sat a while in the same spot on the floor where a body had once unfolded out of him like a plant come up out of the ground, and he thought about all of it: the rolling, and the praying, and the one word in a doorway, and Silas Boyd's lantern, and Munro's fire, and a sheriff standing six feet back from a boy he didn't have the stomach to accuse properly, and a widow who boiled water without asking a single question that wasn't hers to ask, and a tent with his own name turned into an advertisement, and a fire iron he hadn't picked up, and a father who still couldn't, and a mother who finally could.

He didn't pray this time. He didn't feel a want in him large enough to bring to that particular room anymore, which struck him, sitting there, as the truest kind of answer a church could ever have given a boy. Not a body granted, not a wish denied. Just a boy grown large enough in himself that the asking had quietly stopped being necessary.

He walked back out into the sun and down the road, and his shadow, this time of day, fell out long ahead of him rather than behind, stretched over the gravel toward wherever he meant to walk to next, and he found he didn't mind that at all. He understood it slowly, a beat behind when it mattered but arriving all the same, same as he understood most things now: a shadow thrown out ahead of a person only ever shows where they're headed, not where they've been, and a boy who'd spent his whole life being decided about by other people's fear and other people's wonder had finally gotten around to deciding, all on his own, which direction he meant to keep rolling in.

He kept walking. South, toward a mill town and a mending shop and a woman who'd once called him bad at stealing bread and meant it kindly, and he did not look back at the church, or the house, or the county, even once, the whole way there.
