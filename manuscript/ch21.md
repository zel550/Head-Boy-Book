## Chapter Twenty-One

Wendell went to the rail hotel alone in the morning, or meant to. Ruth was three steps behind him the whole walk over, and when he turned to tell her to go back, she only said, "You can waste breath on that if you like. I'll still be walking behind you when you're done," and he understood, the way he'd come to understand most things about her, that there wasn't a version of this argument he was going to win.

Crane received them in the hotel's front parlor, which he'd arranged himself into like a man holding court, Purdy standing near the door in the particular stillness of a dog that's been told to wait but not told to stop wanting to.

"I've thought on your ledger," Wendell said, before Crane could open with any warmth of his own. "I don't owe you a debt. And I don't mean to pay you one, not today, not any day you come knocking on that porch again. You want to tell this town, or the next one, or every town down every road you still work, what kind of boy I am and what kind of house Ruth Calloway keeps — you go on and tell it. I'll stand in the street of every one of those towns myself and tell them what kind of water you sell, and what it costs a crowd to believe in it, and I expect my telling'll travel just as far as yours does, since I've had some practice now at being believed."

He watched it land, watched Crane's easy face do the arithmetic behind it that Wendell had learned to read a long time back, and understood he'd guessed right — that a man whose whole trade ran on being trusted at first glance in a new town every week could not, in fact, afford a boy standing in the last town's street calling him a fraud in the same flat voice that had once sold water for him.

It should have ended there. It very nearly did.

It was Purdy who moved first, not on any word from Crane, just the simple frustration of a man who'd come a long way expecting an easy collection and was leaving instead with nothing, and he crossed the parlor fast and got a hand on Wendell's collar, and Ruth stepped in without a moment's thought for her own balance and shoved at his arm, and Purdy, bigger and unbothered by it, put her down hard against the parlor's iron stove fender with a sound that emptied every other thought clean out of Wendell's head.

What happened next happened faster than Wendell could afterward put in order. He had Purdy off balance and down on one knee against the hearth before he understood he'd moved at all, and there was a fire iron within reach of his hand, close enough that taking it up would have cost him nothing but the length of his own arm, and Purdy was stunned and slow getting up and utterly, completely open to whatever Wendell meant to do next.

He understood, in the half a second he stood there with his hand already halfway to the iron, exactly what he could do with it, and exactly how little would stand between him and doing it — not Crane, who'd gone still and pale by the window, not the parlor's one elderly guest frozen mid-rise from her chair, nothing but Wendell's own hand deciding, of its own accord, to close on the iron or not to.

He thought of his father's one word in a doorway. He thought of a sheriff standing six feet back, too frightened of what he might be to ask him a fair question. He thought, standing there with his hand still open and empty in the air, that he had spent his whole life being decided about by people too afraid to look at him plainly, and that if he closed his hand on that iron now, he would be handing every one of them the proof they'd always wanted, that the strangeness in him had a violence in it after all, worth being afraid of.

He didn't take it up. He stepped back instead, and said, in the flat voice that had told a hundred crowds a hundred true things, "Get him up and get out, or the next witness to what kind of men you are won't be a hotel parlor, it'll be a sheriff," and it should have been enough, and very nearly was.

But the half-second he'd spent not closing his hand on that iron was a half-second Purdy used instead to find his own feet, and he came up faster than Wendell had allowed for and caught him once, hard, across the jaw, before Crane finally stepped between them, not out of mercy, Wendell understood even through the ringing in his own skull, but out of the plain practical fact that an elderly witness was now fully upright and calling for the desk clerk, and a scene like this one was worth less to Crane's ledger than a quiet retreat.

"We'll speak again, Wendell," Crane said, backing toward the door with Purdy in tow, and it was not, this time, delivered warm at all.

Wendell sat down hard on the parlor floor once they'd gone, his jaw already swelling, and found Ruth beside him a moment later, her own shoulder held stiff and wrong from the fall against the fender, and neither one of them said anything for a while, the two of them simply breathing in the wreck of a parlor that had, ten minutes before, belonged to somebody else's ordinary morning entirely.
