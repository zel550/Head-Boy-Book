## Chapter Eight

The widow's name was Vesta Coombs, and she found him the way you find most things you weren't looking for, which is by accident, on her way to check a fence line that hadn't needed checking so much as it had needed an excuse for her to be out walking that morning.

He was sitting against a fence post by then, having made it that far from the creek bed on legs that had decided, somewhere in the walking, that they'd done enough for one night and meant to say so. His hand had stopped bleeding and started doing the other thing instead, the hot swollen thing a cut does when it's been dragged through a dry cornfield and not looked at properly since. He didn't hear her coming. He heard her stop.

"Well," she said, which was, Wendell thought, a fair amount like the first word anybody had ever said about him, all those years back in a room he didn't remember, and he almost laughed at that, except laughing seemed like more than he had in him just then.

She didn't ask him what had happened. She didn't ask him who he was, or where he'd come from, or whether the county's rumors about a strange boy loose in the dry season had anything to do with the boy currently bleeding on her fence post. She looked at him the particular length of time it takes a person to decide a thing is simpler than it's being made out to be, and she said, "You'd best come on to the house before that hand gets any ideas of its own," and that was the whole of her interrogation.

Her house was small and kept the way a house is kept by somebody with time on their hands and nobody left to spend it on. She sat him at her table and boiled water and cleaned the cut with something that stung enough to earn a sound out of him he wasn't proud of, and she wrapped it in a strip of clean cloth torn off something that had probably been a good shirt once, and the whole time she worked she didn't ask him a single question that required an answer longer than yes or no.

"You got people," she said, once, not even lifting it to a question mark, more setting it down between them to see if he'd pick it up.

"Not anymore," Wendell said, the same true-but-not-whole answer he'd given Silas Boyd, weeks and a knife and a good deal of blood ago.

Vesta Coombs nodded like that settled something, though Wendell couldn't have said what, and didn't push on it, and he understood, watching her not push, that she was a woman who'd had her own version of a story nobody in that county asked her about either, some private accounting of her own that had taught her exactly how much a person owes a stranger by way of explanation, which in her figuring was nothing at all.

She fed him for three days. She didn't call it charity and she didn't make a lesson of it. She put him to work splitting a little wood, not because she needed it split so much as because she seemed to understand that a boy recovers faster from a thing when his hands have a job that isn't holding still and thinking about it, and Wendell was grateful for that in a way he didn't have words for yet, the plain animal relief of being useful to somebody who wasn't asking him to be anything else besides.

On the third night, over a supper of beans that tasted better to him than any beans had a right to, he asked her, because the question had been sitting in him since the fence post and he couldn't get it to stay down any longer, "Why'd you take me in? Whole county's near about ready to run me out of it."

She considered that a while, the way she seemed to consider most things, unhurried, like she had more time left than she knew what to do with and saw no cause to waste any of it on a fast answer.

"I don't much go by what a county's ready to do," she said. "Never have. County's ready to do all kinds of things, most of them not worth doing. You were hurt and sitting on my fence line. That's the whole of what I had to go on, and it was enough."

She sent him off on the fourth morning with dry socks, a little money folded into a handkerchief he didn't discover until he was three miles gone, and no questions asked at the door about where he was headed or what he'd do when he got there. Wendell walked a long way that day turning her over in his mind the same careful way he'd turned over Silas Boyd and the sheriff at Loman's Fork and the lanterns in Odell Priest's corn, and what he came to, somewhere past noon with the sun flat and hard on the road, was that Vesta Coombs hadn't fed him because he'd earned it by suffering enough to deserve it, and hadn't fed him despite the rumors either, in some grand gesture that needed the rumors to mean anything. She'd simply found a hurt thing on her fence line and done the plain, unremarkable work of tending it, the way you'd tend anything hurt that crossed your path, and it occurred to him, walking, that this — not the miracle, not the mob, not even Munro's hard-won road wisdom — might be the truest thing he'd learned since leaving his own kitchen table: that decency, when it was real, didn't announce itself as anything special at all. It just got up and boiled the water.
