## Chapter Five

He met Pete Munro at a water tower outside a town whose name Wendell never did catch, on account of Munro talking over the sign as they walked past it, already three sentences into an opinion about railroads that didn't seem to require Wendell's participation so much as his presence.

Munro was old in the specific way the road makes a man old, different from what years alone do. He had the years too, but something on top of them, a kind of weathering, like a fence post that's stood through enough winters to stop bothering to look new. He wore a coat that had been three other coats before it settled on being this one, and he carried nothing that couldn't be carried at a run, which Wendell would come to understand was the whole philosophy of the man, condensed.

"You're the head boy," Munro said, before Wendell had said a word to him, before Wendell had even decided whether this was a man worth saying words to.

Wendell stopped walking. "How'd you know that."

"Word travels faster than boys do," Munro said. "Always has. You'll get used to arriving places that already have an opinion about you." He looked Wendell over, not unkindly, the same weighing look a man gives a mule he's deciding whether to buy. "Ain't nothing wrong with your legs I can see."

"They grew in later," Wendell said, as much of the story as he'd learned to give strangers, a sentence with all the corners sanded off it.

Munro took that plainly, without asking for more than a man was offering. "Well," he said. "They work now, which is the only part of a leg's business that concerns me. Come on. There's a train through here tonight and I mean to be on it, and you look like a boy who's slower at getting up into things than you ought to be, so we'd best start now."

He was not, it turned out, a man given to teaching in the ordinary sense. No sitting a boy down, no lessons laid out in order. He taught like water wears a stone, mostly by wearing at it in passing. Wendell learned to time a jump onto a moving boxcar by watching Munro do it twice and getting shouted at the third time for doing it wrong. He learned which houses in which towns would feed a boy at the back door without asking his business, and which ones would call the sheriff before they'd call him anything at all, and that there was no telling the two kinds of house apart except by having already knocked on the wrong one once.

It was Munro who said the thing about reputation, on the fourth night, over a fire that wasn't big enough to be called a fire so much as an agreement between the two of them and a few sticks.

"You know what you got," Munro said, "that most boys on this road would trade a good pair of boots for?"

"No," Wendell said.

"Ain't nobody can look at you and know what to make of you." Munro said it plainly, like a fact he'd checked, and though Wendell's father used to say things in that same flat register, this landed nothing like his father's words ever had. It landed like something offered instead of something taken away. "That's worth more than you think, out here. A boy that fits a shape people already know, orphan, runaway, thief, half-wit, that boy gets handled according to his shape. Beat, or pitied, or put to work, or turned in, depending which. But a boy don't fit no shape at all, a boy that puts a strangeness in a man's stomach he can't name, that boy a lot of folks'll just leave be. Not out of kindness. Out of not wanting the trouble of figuring out what you are."

"That's not much of a gift," Wendell said. "Feels more like the reason people don't feed me."

"It's both," Munro said, without much sympathy in it, because sympathy wasn't a thing Munro dealt in freely. "Most things worth having out here cut two ways. You'll learn which way's up faster if you quit waiting on somebody to tell you and start using it your own self, 'stead of just carrying it around like a rock in your shoe."

Wendell thought about the barn, and the gun, and the plate of beans that had come after it in the same twenty minutes, and saw that Munro was naming something he'd already half-learned without the words for it.

He rode with Munro the better part of three weeks, through towns whose churches all looked like his own church from a distance and none of them up close, and he picked up the mechanics of the road in pieces, sideways, mixed in with complaints about the railroad and opinions about weather, same as anyone learns something from an old man who isn't trying to be remembered for it. And then one morning outside a town with a grain elevator taller than anything else for twenty miles, Munro simply wasn't there when Wendell woke. No goodbye in it, no ceremony, just an empty patch of ground where a coat-shaped man had been the night before.

Wendell sat with that a while. He'd have liked a goodbye. But he understood, sitting with it, that a man who traveled as light as Munro did wasn't going to weigh himself down with an exit either, and that this too was a kind of lesson, offered sideways same as all the others: the road gives you people the same way it gives you weather, and takes them back the same way, and a boy who wanted to keep something had best learn to hold it while he had it, since holding on after wasn't a thing the road much allowed.
