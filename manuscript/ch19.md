## Chapter Nineteen

The knock came on a Thursday, a little after supper, three raps and a pause and then two more, the particular rhythm of a person who wants to be let in more than they want to be polite about the asking.

Wendell was at the kitchen table finishing a collar for Mrs. Ordway's paying custom, and Ruth was across from him working a hem of her own, and they both went still at the sound of it the same instant, the way two people will when a house that's been quiet a long while suddenly isn't.

"Expecting anybody?" Ruth asked, low.

"No," Wendell said, and found his own voice had gone flat in the particular way Ruth had learned to read as trouble, and he was up and at the window before he'd properly decided to be, looking out at the porch through a gap in the curtain the way he'd once looked out from a hay-loft at a lantern he hadn't invited either.

There were two men on the step. One he didn't know, broad through the shoulders in the way of a man kept around for lifting and little else. The other he knew immediately, even three years and a good deal of road between this porch and the last time he'd seen that particular face by lantern-light behind a tent — Tobias Crane, a little grayer at the temple, dressed finer than Wendell remembered, wearing the same easy smile he kept in reserve for doors he hadn't yet been let through.

"It's him," Wendell said.

Ruth was up and beside him at the window in an instant, her mending still in one hand, and she looked out at the two men on her own porch a long moment before she spoke, and when she did her voice had none of the softness he'd have expected and none of the fear he was feeling plenty of for the both of them. "Well," she said, "he found the street after all. I'll give the man this much — he's patient."

"You should go out the back," Wendell said. "Whatever this is, it's mine to answer, not yours."

"It stopped being only yours the day I let two women in this town believe you were my cousin," Ruth said, and there was no argument left in her voice at all, just a flat finality that matched his own. "I told you once already, I don't much care for being handed a story after the fact instead of being let in on it beforehand. I mean to be standing right here when that door opens, same as you."

The knock came again, patient, unhurried, the knock of a man who has collected on debts before and has learned that a closed door only ever buys the person behind it a little more time, never an escape from the asking.

Wendell looked at Ruth, and Ruth looked back at him, steady in a way that steadied something in him too, and he understood, standing there with his hand not yet on the latch, that whatever waited on the other side of that door had been three years arriving and was not, whatever came of it, going to be settled by staying quiet in a dark kitchen and hoping a knock, unanswered long enough, might simply lose interest and walk back down the porch steps the way it had come.

He opened the door.
