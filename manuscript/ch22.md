## Chapter Twenty-Two

The doctor cost more than the savings in Wendell's boot could cover, and Ruth's besides, and the two of them sat that evening with what was left of a year and a half of careful pennies spread thin across the kitchen table, doing an arithmetic neither of them had wanted to learn.

Her shoulder wasn't broken, the doctor said, only wrenched bad and bruised worse, and would mend with rest she couldn't much afford to take, since Mrs. Ordway's mending work didn't pay a woman for lying still. Wendell's jaw was the same story in a smaller key, swollen, not broken, healing on its own schedule whether he liked its pace or not.

It was the other cost that sat heavier on the table between them, the one no doctor had a bill for. Mrs. Ordway had heard enough of the parlor's story by way of the hotel's help, who talked as freely as anybody in that town ever did, to come to Ruth with a careful, unhappy conversation about the kind of trouble a respectable house couldn't be seen keeping close to. She hadn't put either of them out, not yet, but the ease that had once let Wendell sleep in that shed without a second thought from anybody on the street had gone thin and watchful, air gone thin at a height a person isn't built for.

"I should go," Wendell said, the words costing him as much as anything he'd ever said, sitting across from Ruth with her arm in its sling and her mending, for the first time since he'd known her, sitting untouched in her lap. "You're not putting me out. I see now what staying costs a person standing near me, and I never once let myself add that into the arithmetic before, same as I never told you the size of what I was carrying till it walked up your porch steps."

"I know you should," Ruth said, the plainest thing she'd ever said to him, no softening in it at all, which he was grateful for even as it landed like a stone. "Not forever. I'm not telling you forever, Wendell, and don't you mistake me for saying it. But Crane's not going to forget a boy who cost him twice now, and this town's gone watchful in a way it wasn't a month back, and I've spent three years building a peace here I mean to keep, same as you've spent your whole life looking for one you never got the chance to build. We're not going to build ours on top of a debt that keeps knocking at the door."

"Where would I even go," Wendell said, and heard, saying it, an old boy's voice underneath his own, a twelve-year-old's, the one that had walked out a farmhouse door with a sack and a knife and no plan beyond away.

Ruth considered that a long while, patient with it past the point where most people would've set it down, the same patience she gave a stubborn seam. "You've told me plenty about the road," she said finally. "And plenty about Crane, and Munro, and a widow who fixed your hand once without asking a single question she wasn't owed the answer to. You've told me near everything, Wendell, except the one place you never once mention going back to."

He didn't answer that right away. He hadn't, in three years and a thousand miles of road, let himself think past the leaving of that house, the packed sack and the dirt road and his mother's voice at the window saying you weren't the mistake. He'd built a whole life's worth of habit around not looking back at it, the same careful not-looking his own father had once practiced on him for six years running.

"I don't know what's even left there," he said. "Might be nothing. Might be a man who still can't look at me, no more than he ever could."

"Might be," Ruth agreed. "Or might be your mother, still standing at that same window, wondering same as you've wondered. Either way, Wendell, you can't build a life on top of ground you've never once gone back to check the shape of. I learned that much from losing a leg: you don't get to skip looking at the wound just because looking hurts worse than the losing did. You go back, and you look at it plain, and then you come back to me, if there's a coming back in you, and we'll figure the rest from there, once Crane's had time to lose interest in a boy who isn't standing on my porch to be found."

It was not, Wendell understood, a sending-away. It was the opposite of one, dressed in the only clothes Ruth Calloway ever seemed to dress a hard true thing in: plain, unsoftened, and meant.

He left three days later, once her shoulder had mended enough that she could manage the mending alone, walking north this time instead of away, toward a house he hadn't seen in three years and a man he wasn't sure he had anything left to say to, and a mother he hoped, more than he let himself say out loud, was still standing at that same window.
