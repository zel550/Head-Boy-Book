## Chapter Three

He was twelve when it finally gave.

Not from any one thing. That's a thing people want, later, when he tells it — they want the fight, the slammed door, the single terrible sentence that broke the family clean in two, the way you want a bone to have one obvious break instead of a dozen small ones. But it wasn't like that. It was three hundred suppers eaten in near silence. It was a father who'd shake his hand like Wendell was a man he'd just met at a feed auction, formal and brief and already looking past him at the door. It was the town, too, which had gotten tired of being amazed and moved on to being suspicious, the way towns do, so that by the time Wendell was ten there were already people who crossed the street a little when they saw him coming, not out of meanness exactly, more the way you'd cross the street from a dog that had bitten somebody once, even a dog you liked.

The last supper he ate in that house, his father finally said something. It wasn't much. Wendell had knocked over his milk reaching for the bread — a body thing, an arms-and-elbows thing, the kind of small clumsiness that any boy his age would've been forgiven without a second thought — and his father looked at the spreading white puddle on the tablecloth like it confirmed something he'd suspected all along.

"Careless," his father said. Not loud. Just tired, and certain, the way a man states a fact he's checked more than once.

"It was an accident," his mother said.

"Everything about him is an accident," his father said, and then seemed to hear it, and didn't take it back, which was its own kind of answer.

Wendell didn't cry. He'd cried plenty over smaller things — the stove, the falling, a hundred humiliations of learning a body from scratch — but this one landed somewhere past crying, in the flat quiet place where a person starts doing arithmetic instead of feeling anything. He looked at his father. He looked at his mother, who wouldn't meet his eyes, not because she agreed but because she'd run clean out of ways to defend a marriage from the inside of it. And he thought: *I did this. Or the church did. Or God did, if you want to go all the way up the chain. But whoever did it, this house isn't built to hold what I turned into, and I'm the only one in it who can walk away from that math without it costing him everything he's got left.*

He left before it was light, the way you leave when you've decided a thing needs no witnesses and no permission, taking a canvas sack with two shirts in it, a knife his uncle had given him for no occasion at all, and half a loaf of bread he felt guilty about and took anyway.

His mother was awake. Mothers generally are, the night their sons decide something like this, whether or not anybody told them the decision out loud. She was standing at the kitchen window when he came through in his coat, and she didn't try to stop him, which he understood later was the hardest thing she ever did, harder by far than anything that had happened at that church.

"You'll need more than a knife and some bread," she said.

"I'll get what I need."

"That's not a plan, that's a hope."

"It's what I've got."

She looked at him a long moment — this boy she'd carried on her hip for six years as a talking head and then watched, terrified and grateful in equal parts, grow into a whole clumsy loud-footed human being who ate too fast and fell down running and had, somewhere in the last year, gotten taller than her — and she didn't tell him not to go, because she'd learned by then that some doors don't stay closed for being told to, they only stay closed until the hinge finally gives, and hers had given a long time before he ever packed the sack.

"Whatever you find out there," she said, "don't let it teach you you were a mistake. You weren't the mistake. He just never learned how to love a boy who could leave the room."

Wendell didn't have an answer for that, not yet, not at twelve with a knife and half a loaf of bread and the whole indifferent dark of the county ahead of him. He just nodded, the way you nod at something you know you'll need to understand later and can't afford to stop and understand now, and he went out the door and down the porch steps — walking, this time, on his own two legs, past the exact spot where he used to roll — and out onto the road, headed nowhere in particular except away, which at twelve years old, with nothing behind him but a house that couldn't hold him and nothing ahead of him but the length of the state, felt for a little while like the same thing as headed somewhere.
