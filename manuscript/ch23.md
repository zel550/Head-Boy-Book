## Chapter Twenty-Three

The house looked smaller than he remembered it, same as places from childhood always do once a person's grown enough to measure them against a body that's stopped changing size every year.

He stood at the end of the dirt road a long while before he walked it, the same road he'd rolled down at seven years old and walked down at twelve, and he noted, same as he noted most things now, that his shadow fell behind him in the morning light instead of ahead, which struck him as worth remembering though he couldn't yet have said why.

His mother was at the clothesline. She was older, grayer at the temple than he'd carried her in his memory, but she was standing exactly where he'd last properly seen her happy, all those years back, dropping a whole basket in the dirt at the sight of a boy come walking up the road on legs he'd never owned. She saw him a long way off this time too, and she went still the same way, and watching her hand come up slow to her mouth, Wendell understood she'd been doing her own arithmetic every single day he'd been gone, same as he'd been doing his.

She didn't drop the basket this time. She set it down careful, a woman setting down a thing she means to pick back up, and she crossed the yard at something just short of a run, and she held him a long while without either of them saying anything at all. Standing in his mother's arms in the yard he'd been born a head in, Wendell found that some part of him had been carrying this exact moment folded up small in his chest the whole three years, waiting on a place to unfold.

"You're taller," she said, finally, pulling back to look at him proper. "And you've got a mark on your jaw wants explaining, and a look about you I don't recognize yet, but I expect I will, given time."

"It's a long telling," Wendell said.

"I've got nothing but time now," she said, "and a kitchen, and I don't believe I've stopped setting a plate for you at supper the whole while you were gone, foolish as that sounds, so you'd best come tell it to me over a full one."

His father was there too, home early from a mill that had cut its hours the year before, sitting in the same chair that faced away from the kitchen, and he turned it around when he heard the door, slower than Wendell remembered him moving, older by more than three years' worth, a man who'd gone somewhere quieter inside himself in the time between.

He looked at Wendell a long moment. Wendell looked back, standing in that doorway a grown boy instead of a whole one arriving for the first time, and understood he was waiting for something without having decided in advance what shape he needed it to come in: an apology, maybe, or an explanation, or even just the one word reversed, a "yes" to balance out the "no" that had cost them both so much.

"You've done some growing," his father said instead, true, and also, Wendell understood immediately, the whole of what his father had to offer by way of a greeting.

"I have," Wendell said.

His father nodded, and looked at him a while longer, and something moved behind his face that might have been regret or might only have been the ordinary weariness of a man whose mill had cut his hours and whose body had gotten old faster than he'd planned for. Wendell understood he wasn't going to be able to tell the two apart from the outside of the man, same as he never had been able to.

"I still can't," his father said, finally, quiet, not cruel in it this time, just tired and plain, the same tone he'd used the night he said careless over a spilled glass of milk. "I look at you now, grown, gone out and come back on your own two legs having seen God knows what out there, and I still don't know how to be in a room with the boy you turned into. I don't reckon I ever will. That's not yours to fix. I've had three years to try fixing it myself and I haven't managed it, so I don't imagine you standing in that door's going to do what three years alone in this chair couldn't."

Wendell sat with that a long moment, working it over same as he worked over everything, and found it landed on him differently than it once would have. Not as a verdict on what he was worth, which is how it had landed at twelve years old over a puddle of spilled milk, but as a plain fact about a man's own limits, sad and small and entirely separate from Wendell's own accounting of himself.

"I didn't come back needing you to," Wendell said, and saying it, understood it was the truest thing he'd said in that house since the word arms and legs first left his seven-year-old mouth at a kitchen sink. "I came back to see her. And to see was there anything left here worth carrying forward. I expect there's her. I don't expect there's much of you, and I find that doesn't break me the way it once would have."

His father didn't answer that, and didn't need to. Wendell turned from the doorway and went to sit at his mother's table instead, where a plate had, in fact, been kept ready for him the whole three years, foolish as she'd called it, and he ate a full supper in that kitchen for the first time since he was twelve years old, and did not look back at the chair facing away from the kitchen even once, the whole meal through.
