## Chapter Eleven

Crane put him up on the platform two nights later, in a shirt a size too big for him that had been chosen, Wendell understood only once he was standing in it under the lantern light, precisely because it made him look smaller and younger than he was, a detail nobody had asked his opinion on and one he filed next to the others without comment, the way he'd learned to.

The banner behind him had been repainted again. It read, in the same red letters as before, THE BOY WHO PRAYED HIMSELF WHOLE, and under that, smaller, WITNESS AND BELIEVE, and Wendell stood beneath his own name turned into an advertisement and felt something in his chest that he couldn't yet decide was pride or theft, on account of it being, he'd come to think, some of both at once.

Crane introduced him with the same gathering hush he'd used on the woman with the cane, working the crowd down to a stillness you could have heard a coin drop in, and then he stepped back, which surprised Wendell — he'd expected to be talked over, the way the woman had been talked over, and instead found himself standing alone in the lantern light with a few hundred faces turned up at him, waiting.

So he told it. Not Crane's version, near enough — the true one, the one he'd given Crane by the cashbox, his mother's part left whole and unbent exactly as he'd bargained for. He told about the roll down the porch steps and the mile and a half of gravel, and the church empty on a Tuesday, and the plain words he'd said because he didn't know any fancier ones, and the sound like wet rope, and he found, standing up there, that the flat practical voice he'd used his whole life to say the hardest things that happened to him was, it turned out, exactly the voice a few hundred strangers wanted to hear a miracle told in — no ornament on it, nothing embroidered, just a boy saying a true and terrible and wonderful thing had happened to him in the same tone he might've used to report the weather, and the crowd took that plainness for a kind of proof, the way people always will, reasoning that a story too strange to have been made up must not have been.

He was good at it. That was the part that unsettled him most, walking back behind the tent afterward with the crowd's noise still ringing in his ears and Crane's hand clapped warm on his shoulder in a way no hand had touched him since his own mother's — he was good at it, better than he'd let himself expect, and there was a particular quiet horror in discovering that the worst thing that had ever happened in his own kitchen could be stood up and told for money and land, every time, exactly where he aimed it.

"Didn't I tell you," Crane said, counting the night's take by lantern light after, water bottles moving fast enough that two boys Crane kept on for the purpose could barely fetch them from the wagon quick enough. "Didn't I tell you it was a testimony and not just a story. You felt that, up there. I watched you feel it."

"I felt something," Wendell allowed.

"That's the Spirit moving through a true thing," Crane said, "same as it moved through you at that church, and there isn't a soul in that crowd tonight who'll go home unchanged for having heard it, and there isn't a thing wrong with a boy getting paid for carrying a gift like that from town to town, same as a preacher gets paid, same as a doctor gets paid. You're not selling anything false. You're only letting people pay for something true, which is a kindness to them, not a con."

Wendell turned that over a long while that night, lying in the cot Crane had given him in the back of the wagon, listening to the tent canvas breathe in the wind the way a live thing breathes. He wanted to believe Crane's arithmetic, because it made the money in his pocket and the full plate at supper and the first roof he'd slept under two nights running in longer than he could remember all sit easier in him than they otherwise would have. And some part of it, he thought, might even be true. But he noticed, too, that he'd left out one thing in his telling that night without Crane so much as asking him to — he hadn't said, standing up there, that his father had walked out the door ninety seconds after seeing him whole and never fully come back. He'd sanded that corner off on his own, without being told to, because a story that ended in abandonment didn't sell water near so well as one that ended in wonder, and he lay in the dark a long time with that small, voluntary omission sitting on his chest like a stone he'd picked up and put in his own pocket, nobody's hand on it but his.
