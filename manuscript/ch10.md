## Chapter Ten

They talked past midnight, Crane sitting on an upturned crate with the cashbox closed between his boots like a thing he meant to keep an eye on even while he wasn't counting it, and Wendell standing because nobody had offered him a place to sit and he'd learned not to take one uninvited.

He told it plain, the way he told everything, the flat practical voice doing the work it always did — the head, the roll to the church, the body unfolding out of him like a plant come up out of the ground, his father's one word in the doorway. Crane didn't interrupt him once. He had the particular stillness of a man doing arithmetic behind his face while his face did something else entirely, and when Wendell finished, Crane sat with it a while before he spoke, which Wendell would learn was itself a piece of his showmanship, a silence built to make whatever came after sound like it had cost him something to arrive at.

"Son," Crane said, "I have sold a great many stories in my time, most of them not near so true as the one you just told me, and I want you to understand I mean that as a professional compliment and not an insult to either of us. What you've got isn't a story. It's a testimony. There's a difference, and the difference is worth money."

"I didn't tell it to you for money," Wendell said.

"No," Crane agreed, easy as anything, "you told it to me because you're hungry and tired of a road that can't decide what to make of you, which is a different reason and a better one, and it doesn't change what the story's worth once it's told." He opened the cashbox, not all the way, just enough to let Wendell see there was more in it than a Wednesday collection plate could ever have held, and closed it again. "Here's my offer, plain. You stand up under my tent and tell that story, same as you told it to me, near enough, and I'll feed you, and I'll bed you, and I'll cut you a third of what the water brings in on the nights you tell it, which on a good night in a town this size is more money than most grown men in this county see in a month."

"Near enough," Wendell said. "What's the near-enough part."

Crane smiled the smile Wendell would come to know well, the one that cost him nothing and was worth believing anyhow. "A story's got to fit the tent it's told under, son. Folks come to my tent for a certain kind of wonder, and I mean to give it to them, and there's details in a raw true telling that don't serve the wonder so much as they serve accuracy, which are not, whatever the schoolteachers tell you, the same thing at all times."

"My mother stays out of it," Wendell said. He hadn't known he was going to say it until he'd said it, but once it was out he understood it was the one thing he wasn't going to bend on, the way a man discovers the load-bearing wall of a house only when somebody suggests taking it down. "You can dress the rest up however sells water. She doesn't get dressed up. She doesn't get changed. What she said, she said, and what she did, she did, and I won't stand under any tent and tell it different."

Crane looked at him a long moment, the arithmetic working again behind the stillness, and Wendell understood he was being weighed the same way Silas Boyd had weighed him in a lantern's light and the sheriff at Loman's Fork had weighed him from six feet back, except this time the scale wasn't tipping toward danger or mercy so much as toward usefulness, which was its own kind of thing to be weighed for, and not, Wendell thought, entirely worse than the others.

"Your mother stays out of it," Crane said, agreeing the way a man agrees to a term he's already planning to work around later, though Wendell wouldn't understand that for some weeks yet. "You've got a shrewd streak in you, son, under all that flat talk. I like that. Makes for a better partnership than a boy who'll swallow whatever I hand him."

"I'm not your partner," Wendell said. "I'm a boy telling a true story for a third of a water bill."

"Call it whatever settles easiest," Crane said, and put his hand out, and Wendell looked at it a moment before he took it, because a hand offered by a man like Crane was, he understood even then, a kind of contract that no paper could improve on or protect him from, and he shook it anyway, because a third of a water bill was a third of a water bill, and a boy who'd slept in barns and ditches and drought-country creek beds for two years had learned that a man does not always get to wait for the terms he'd prefer before he takes the ones in front of him.

They shook on it under the last doused lantern, and somewhere behind Crane's easy smile Wendell felt, for the first time since the church, a door closing that he hadn't entirely meant to walk all the way through.
