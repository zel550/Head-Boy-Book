## Chapter Seven

It hadn't rained in that county in six weeks when Wendell walked into it, and a place that hasn't had rain in six weeks is a place that has stopped being reasonable about most things.

He didn't know that going in. He knew it was hot and the corn looked wrong, curled up on itself like a hand gone cold even in summer, and he knew the ground had turned the color of a scab everywhere it wasn't planted. What he didn't know was that a farmer named Odell Priest had lost a calf three days before to something the vet couldn't put a name to, and that a Tetlow girl had come down with a fever the same week, and that in a place already frightened of its own dying fields, two unexplained things arriving close together will go looking for a third thing to blame them on. A man with a toothache goes looking for the tooth with his tongue, over and over, whether or not the looking does him any good. A county with two mysteries goes looking the same way.

Wendell was the third thing. He didn't have to do anything to become it. He only had to arrive.

He felt it start slow, before he could put a name to it: a look held a beat too long at the general store, a woman pulling her child in by the shoulder as he passed on the road, a man on a porch who stopped rocking his chair entirely and just watched him go by, not saying a word, which was worse somehow than if he had. By the second day he understood he ought to leave, and by the second night he understood he'd waited too long to decide that on his own terms.

They came for him a little after midnight, eight or nine men with lanterns, which was its own kind of terrible, since a man with a lantern at midnight has already decided the dark isn't going to stop him from whatever he means to do. He heard them before he saw them: dogs first, then boots, then a voice he didn't know saying his description out loud to the others like a charm, that's him, that's the one, come up out of nowhere same week as the Tetlow girl took sick. Wendell was up and running before his own mind had finished deciding to run, his legs doing the thing legs are for before the rest of him caught up to the wisdom of it.

He ran through Odell Priest's dead corn, which cut at him worse than good corn would have, dry stalks raking his arms and his face in the dark, and he went down once, hard, on a furrow he didn't see, and came up with his palm opened along a rock and blood in it he wouldn't properly look at until later. The knife his uncle had given him, the one he'd carried the whole way out of his own county for no better reason than that it had been given to him for no reason at all, worked its way out of his belt somewhere in that field and was gone. He didn't go back for it. A boy stopping to hunt in the dark for a knife is a boy who gets caught, and getting caught that night was not a thing with an ending he wanted to find out the shape of.

He ran until the lanterns behind him were smaller than the stars were, and then he ran a while longer than that, because fear doesn't stop on a schedule just because the danger technically has. He came up finally against a dry creek bed and fell into it more than he lay down in it, and there he stayed until first light, holding his cut hand against his shirt and listening to his own breathing like it belonged to somebody else he was worried about.

Lying there, he understood that what Munro had told him at that thin little fire, that a strangeness folks can't name will as often as not make them leave a man be, had a companion truth riding right alongside it that Munro hadn't gotten around to mentioning. Maybe he hadn't needed to, being an old man nobody had ever mistaken for the cause of a sick child or a dead calf. The same not-being-able-to-place-him that had gotten Wendell a plate of beans in one county and a night's grace in another had very nearly gotten him killed in this one, over a fever and a dead animal he'd had no more to do with than the moon did. There was no rule he could find, working it over in the gray light with his hand throbbing in his shirt, that told a boy in advance which one he was walking into. There was only the walking in, and then the finding out.
