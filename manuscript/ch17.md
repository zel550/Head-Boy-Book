## Chapter Seventeen

The trouble started, as trouble in a boardinghouse town generally does, with the neighbors.

Mrs. Ordway had let it be known, gently at first and then less gently, that a grown boy sleeping in her shed and taking his suppers with a young unmarried woman was not an arrangement the street was going to leave uncommented on forever, whatever the innocence of it. Ruth had handled it quietly, by way of a small useful lie, which Wendell was coming to understand was how she handled most things that threatened the life she'd built.

She'd told two of the neighbor women, and let it travel from there, that Wendell was a cousin of hers, come up from her mother's people downstate, staying a spell while he found work. It wasn't a large lie. It cost nobody anything. And it had bought her, Wendell realized only once he understood what she'd done, several months of exactly the ordinary peace she'd been building since the accident, a peace he'd been living inside of without once asking what it was propped up on.

He found out about it secondhand, from Mrs. Ordway herself, who mentioned "your cousin's chores" to him in passing as though it were simply a fact of the household. He said nothing at the time, but that evening, mending a shirt at the kitchen table, he asked Ruth about it plain.

"You told them I was kin."

"I told them what kept the both of us out of a conversation neither one of us wanted to have," Ruth said, not looking up from her own work. "It's not a large thing, Wendell."

"It's a story," he said, "no different from any I ever told for Crane. You built one about me without asking, to serve a use of your own, and I never even knew I was in it."

She did look up at that, and something in her face went sharp in a way he hadn't seen on her before, not since the first day in the alley. "That's not the same thing and you know it. I didn't sell your story to a crowd for coin. I told two women a small kindness of a lie so a fourteen-year-old boy sleeping in my shed didn't cost me the one respectable situation I've managed to build for myself in this town since I lost the leg. You want to be angry at somebody for building a story around you without your say-so, you've got a whole tent full of better reasons than me to be angry at."

"I never said I was angry."

"You didn't have to. You've got a voice that goes flat right before you mean something, and it's gone flat since you sat down." She set her mending in her lap, and Wendell understood he was about to hear something she'd been carrying a while and hadn't found the moment to say. "You've been in this house four months and you've never once told me the whole of what you left behind on that road, only pieces, the parts that make a good telling. I know there's a man somewhere who's got a grudge on you, because you flinch at strangers same as you did the first week, worse some days, and I've never asked, because I figured it was yours to tell when you meant to. But I've got a life here I built with my own two hands and one leg, Wendell, and if that grudge ever walks up this street looking for you, it's not only your trouble it comes knocking on. It's mine too, and Mrs. Ordway's, and I never got a vote on taking that risk into my house. You did that, same as I told two women a small lie to protect what little I've got. We've both been building stories to keep the peace. I only did mine out loud."

Wendell sat with that a long while, the shirt gone still in his hands. He was coming to understand, slowly and a beat behind when it mattered but arriving all the same, that he'd let himself believe, these four good months, that staying somewhere meant the road's old arithmetic, the coin-flip of danger and mercy, had simply stopped applying to him. It hadn't stopped. He'd only stopped watching it, the plain forgetting of a debt still owed once the collector's gone quiet a while, and Ruth had been carrying the weight of that forgetting right alongside him without ever being told its size.

"His name's Crane," he said, finally, and told her the whole of it, the tent and the water and the mother he wouldn't lie about and the parting words behind the wagons in the dark, and Ruth listened without once softening her face into the kind of pity he'd spent a lifetime learning to flinch from, which he was grateful for more than he had words to say.

"Well," she said, when he'd finished, "now I know what I signed my name to, same as you ought to have let me sooner. That's the whole of what I wanted. Not for you to have no past. Just for me to be let in on the size of it, before it comes walking up my street uninvited."
