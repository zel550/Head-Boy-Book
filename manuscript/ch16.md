## Chapter Sixteen

Wendell stayed in the mill town longer than he'd stayed anywhere since Crane's tent, which surprised him some, since staying wasn't a thing he'd let himself get much practice at. Ruth didn't invite him to. She simply kept not telling him to leave, which in her case amounted to near enough the same thing, and Mrs. Ordway, who ran the boardinghouse on a schedule Ruth swore by and grumbled about in the same breath, let him sleep in the wood shed for a nickel a night and a promise to keep the stove fed, which he was glad enough to give.

It was Ruth who taught him the mending, mostly because she couldn't stand to watch him sit idle of an evening with his hands doing nothing, the same restlessness he'd once described to his own mother as legs with no cure but standing up.

"You hold the needle like it's going to bite you," she said, the first evening she put one in his hand.

"Never held one before."

"Well, it shows." But she showed him anyway, patient in a way that didn't match the rest of her manner, guiding his big clumsy fingers through a hem, the same care you'd use guiding a colt's legs under it. By the end of a week he could set a straight seam, if not a fast one, and by the end of a month Mrs. Ordway had started handing him small jobs of his own, paid in pennies but paid.

"This is a life," Ruth told him one evening, thinking aloud more than teaching, with him in the room to think aloud at. "Not a grand one. But it's a room I pay for with money I earned honest, and work I know how to do, and a town that's stopped looking twice at my leg on account of I've been walking through it on the same leg for two years now and haven't given them a fresh reason to stare. You've been on the road three years and I don't believe you've ever once tried building a thing like that. Only running from what you didn't have."

"Didn't know how," Wendell said. It was true, and true things generally cost him something to say out loud.

"I'm not saying it to shame you," she said. "I'm saying it because I mean to teach you, same as the mending. A boy can learn to stay somewhere just like he learns a seam. It isn't a gift some people are born with and others aren't. It's just a thing nobody showed you."

He gave her something back for it, though it took him longer to understand he had anything worth giving. It was a Tuesday, and he noted that, as he'd noted most Tuesdays since the church, when a man came around the boardinghouse asking after a room, well-dressed, well-spoken, offering more than Mrs. Ordway usually got for a week's stay. Wendell, sitting on the porch step mending a collar, watched the man a full minute before he said anything at all.

"Don't take his money," he told Ruth, once the man had gone inside to look the room over.

"Why not. It's good money."

"Because he asked Mrs. Ordway three separate times whether there were other boarders in the house before he asked to see the room itself, and a man who wants to know who else is watching before he wants to know what he's paying for isn't planning to be an easy boarder, whatever his money looks like going in." He'd learned to read that particular kind of caution on the road, the studying-the-room-before-committing-to-it look he'd seen on sheriffs and mob-men and Crane alike, each one deciding in advance how much trouble a place might cost them before they stepped fully into it.

Ruth sat with that, and turned the man away that evening on some excuse about the room already being spoken for, and three weeks later heard from a boarder two houses down that the same man had been run out of a rooming house across town over money he owed and wouldn't pay and a fight he'd started over the owing of it.

"Well," she said to Wendell, handing him back the collar he'd near finished, "I suppose the road taught you something worth having after all, same as I always figured the mending would."

It was, Wendell thought, the first time since his own mother that somebody had found a use for something in him that had nothing to do with what a crowd or a jailhouse or a tent full of strangers wanted to make of him. He sat with that a long while after she'd gone up to bed, working it over as he worked over most things, and found, for once, he didn't have a single hard edge left in it to worry at.
