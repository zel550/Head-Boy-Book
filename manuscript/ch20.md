## Chapter Twenty

"Wendell," Crane said, warm as ever, as though three years and a ruined night's collection were nothing more than a pause in a conversation neither of them had ever meant to end. "You've filled out some. Road must've agreed with you, this last stretch."

"What do you want," Wendell said.

"Now, that's no way to greet an old partner." Crane's eyes moved past him, unhurried, to Ruth standing at Wendell's shoulder, and something in his face catalogued her the way he'd catalogued a crowd a hundred times from a platform, sizing up what she was worth to the conversation. "And who's this. Cousin, I'm told, round this town. Word does travel, same as ever."

"She's got nothing to do with what's between us," Wendell said.

"She's got a house you're sleeping in and a name you've been borrowing to keep the neighbors easy," Crane said, "which makes her a good deal more to do with it than you'd like, son, and I'd think on that before you take a tone with me on her porch." He let that sit a moment, the old showman's pause, before he came to the business of it. "I've come about a debt. You cost me a night's take in front of several hundred witnesses, and word of that night traveled ahead of me down every road I've worked since, and I've had a harder go of my ministry ever since on account of it. I've done the arithmetic. A year's board and keep, plus what that one night cost me in trade I never fully recovered, comes to a sum I expect you and your cousin here can put together between you, given she's got a room and a job and you've clearly landed someplace soft."

"I don't owe you a debt," Wendell said. "You fed me a year for telling a story that made you rich. I paid for my keep a hundred times over standing on that platform."

"That's one way of reckoning it," Crane agreed, easy as anything, "and I won't say it hasn't got some truth in it. But I didn't come here to argue accounting with you on a porch, son. I came here because a man in my position learns that a debt collected quiet, between old partners, is a good deal more pleasant for everybody than a debt collected loud, in a town where a certain amount could be said, to the right ears, about a strange boy and a crippled girl keeping house together under a lie about kinship, and how a town that's grown comfortable with that lie might feel, once it's shown otherwise." He smiled the smile that cost him nothing. "I've no wish to make trouble for you, Wendell. I've only got a ledger that wants balancing, and I find people balance ledgers a good deal faster once they understand what it costs them not to."

The broad man beside Crane hadn't said a word the whole exchange, hadn't needed to. His silence did its own work, the particular quiet of a man kept around expressly so that other men's words would be taken seriously.

Ruth's hand had gone still on the doorframe beside Wendell's, and when she spoke her voice was steadier than Wendell's own had been, though he could feel, standing close to her, that it cost her something to keep it that way. "You're threatening to spread a story about my house to a town I've spent three years building a life in, over a debt you invented on the spot on my own porch."

"I'm offering," Crane said, "a chance to settle a private matter privately, which is more courtesy than most men in my position would extend, and I'd think on that kindly before you decide I'm the villain of this particular telling."

Wendell felt something in his chest go very still and very cold, the same stillness he'd once felt running through Odell Priest's dead corn with lanterns behind him, except this time there was nowhere to run to that wouldn't mean leaving Ruth standing alone on the step with whatever Crane meant to do next.

"Give us till morning," Wendell said, "to think on what we've got to give you." He said it not because he meant to pay Crane a single cent of an invented debt, but because a night bought was a night to think, and thinking, on the road, had kept him alive a good deal longer than courage ever had.

Crane considered that, and nodded, slow, satisfied the particular way a man is satisfied when a negotiation has gone exactly the direction he expected it to. "Morning, then," he said. "I'll be at the rail hotel, same as any respectable man passing through. Don't make me come looking for you again, Wendell. I don't believe your cousin's nerves could stand a second visit as pleasant as this one."

He touched his hat to Ruth, the courtesy of it worse somehow than any threat could have been, and walked back down the porch steps with the broad man a pace behind him. Wendell and Ruth stood in the open doorway a long while after, watching the dark where the two of them had gone, neither one saying anything at all, because there wasn't, just yet, anything to say that the night hadn't already said plainly enough on its own.
