# WENDELL ROLLS (working title: THE HEAD) — Project Plan

*A comprehensive plan synthesizing marketing/positioning, craft/structure, illustration production, and disability-representation review. Compiled from four independent specialist passes plus original research on comp titles and current AI image tools.*

---

## 1. The Concept, Stated Plainly

A boy in a rural, Depression-flavored American county is born as literally nothing but a head — no body, the neck sealed like an old scar. He grows up rolling to get around, loved fiercely by his mother, unlooked-at by his father. At seven, following his mother's bitter dare to "ask God yourself," he prays in an empty church and a full body unfolds out of his neck-stump like a plant breaking ground. His father cannot survive this — he leaves not because his son is now disabled, but because his son is no longer *containable*: "I did the head. I can't do the boy." By twelve, isolated by both his father's absence and a town that's curdled from wonder into suspicion, Wendell runs away and becomes a drifter, navigating a world that keeps trying to hand him a label — miracle, fraud, monster — instead of a name.

The origin is a dark Bulgarian joke trope (a baby born as "just a head") — I searched Bulgarian-language sources at length and could not surface a verbatim indexed version of the specific joke; this style of bleak birth-defect joke lives mostly in oral culture, not searchable text. That's fine — you're not adapting a joke, you're using its shape as a seed for something much larger, the way a fable or an urban legend seeds a novel. The title's pun (a literal head / a "head boy" as a school's top honor) is doing real work regardless of the joke's exact wording.

## 2. Genre & Positioning

**Category:** Literary fiction with Southern Gothic / American folk-fable inflection — closer to *fabulism* than "magical realism," because the transformation isn't ambient texture, it's the plot engine. In a pitch: **upmarket literary fiction, illustrated.**

**Real, load-bearing comp titles:**
- ***Ingram*** (Louis C.K.) — the anchor comp. Deadpan-tender odyssey structure, a resourceless boy forced onto the road, rural American hardship, harrowing-yet-hopeful register. This is a real, traditionally published (Simon & Schuster/BenBella) novel and NYT bestseller — a legitimate comp to cite, not just a vibe.
- ***A Monster Calls*** (Patrick Ness) — the single best illustrated-literary-fiction comp: a literal monster, grief, an absent/failing parent, tender-horror illustration (Jim Kay), adult-serious despite crossover packaging.
- ***Geek Love*** (Katherine Dunn) — bodily difference, a family that can't fully hold its own child, dark humor riding on top of real love.
- ***The Tin Drum*** (Günter Grass) — a child's body as both metaphor and literal plot mechanism, folk-tale/political fable structure.
- ***Big Fish*** (Daniel Wallace) — Southern tall-tale voice, father-son myth-making, small-town Americana.
- Flannery O'Connor's story collections — the deadpan-grotesque-religious register this book is explicitly channeling.

**Audience:** Literary-fiction readers 30–55 who like Southern Gothic/fabulist voice, plus a real crossover into upper-YA/adult-crossover readers (16+), the way *A Monster Calls* pulls that audience in despite adult-dark content. Strong book-club potential *if* marketing foregrounds the father/family/belonging throughline over the shock premise.

---

## 3. Title

Neither existing working title survives scrutiny. **"THE HEAD"** reads clinical — could be mistaken for a thriller. **"HEAD BOY"** collides badly with the British/Commonwealth "head boy" (school prefect) meaning, which is a real international-market and SEO liability.

**Recommended title: `WENDELL ROLLS`** — a character-name title in the *Ingram* tradition; "rolls" is literal (how he moves as a head) and doubles as momentum/fate/dice-roll connotation. This is the title used across this project's files.

Runner-up alternatives, if you want to reconsider: *The Boy Who Prayed for Legs* (states the hook plainly, very marketable, less literary), *Miracle Boy* (ironic once the town turns), *Just a Head* (leans into the joke-origin but risks feeling glib for the book's ambitions).

## 4. Path to Market

An unknown debut pitching a headless-infant premise *plus* an AI-illustration ask is a hard sell cold to trad publishers — they're cost-averse on illustrated adult fiction, and AI-generated art is currently a live landmine for trad houses (disclosure requirements, award-eligibility questions, reviewer backlash).

**Recommended sequence, in order:**
1. **Serialize free on Substack (or similar) first.** Cheapest possible market test — does this land with real readers before you've spent real money on art? This also builds an email list that becomes your buyer base for step 2.
2. **Kickstarter-funded illustrated limited edition next**, once you have an engaged base. Real precedent: illustrated fairy-tale-register novels (T. Kingfisher/Ursula Vernon) and large-scale novel crowdfunds (Brandon Sanderson) both show crowdfunding audiences specifically reward "beautiful object + weird premise + author-direct voice" — and self-select past the AI-art controversy that would sink you with a mainstream trad-review audience.
3. **Trad publishing as upside optionality, not the plan.** If the serialized/crowdfunded version generates real buzz, *that's* the leverage for an agent query — not a cold submission.

**Marketing hooks, ranked:**
1. Lead with the father-can't-look-at-his-son thread, not the premise — more universally relatable, undersells the "gimmick" risk.
2. "In the vein of *Ingram*" — direct, credible comp-citation.
3. The transformation scene as the single marketing image (cover/key art).
4. "For readers of *A Monster Calls*" — gift-book/crossover angle.
5. **The Bulgarian-joke origin story is press color, not a lead hook.** Great answer to "where did this come from," bad headline — it risks making a literary novel sound like a bit, and invites "so what's the actual joke" scrutiny the source can't satisfy.

**Honest risk assessment:** the biggest critical risk isn't marketing, it's the manuscript itself — a "prayer grants a disabled body its missing parts" premise reads as ableist wish-fulfillment unless Wendell's interiority as a head is written with full dignity *before* the transformation, and unless the text keeps working against the "cure" reading throughout (see §6). Sustaining deadpan-Gothic-comic tone across 45–55k words is much harder than three strong chapters prove. AI-illustration will draw disproportionate backlash independent of prose quality. And hitching to *Ingram* ties you to Louis C.K.'s public persona either way — visibility, but baggage too.

## 5. Structure & Craft Direction

**Thematic thesis:** *Wendell must become a person — with will, appetite, and the capacity to leave — in a world that only knows how to receive him as a symbol: miracle, fraud, monster, tragedy, never simply "Wendell."* The father could love the head — static, carriable, safely other — and cannot love the boy, who can walk, run, and leave. The prayer doesn't "fix" Wendell; it reveals that wholeness forfeits the only love that had adapted to him. That reversal is the engine — protect it, don't resolve it into a moral.

**Scale:** an illustrated literary novella, *A Monster Calls* scale. **24 chapters, ~1,200–2,000 words each, ~45,000–55,000 words total.** One illustration per chapter. See `01-outline.md` for the full beat sheet.

**Five books:**
1. **The House** (Ch. 1–3, existing) — birth, prayer, transformation, the father's collapse, the leaving.
2. **Apprenticeship in Being Seen** (Ch. 4–9) — early road encounters; the "monster" label is established doing both work: protecting him from casual cruelty, and isolating him from real belonging.
3. **The Exhibition** (Ch. 10–14) — a carnival/revival set piece, used deliberately as critique of the spectacle economy, not as spectacle itself — Wendell *chooses* to sell his own story, then confronts what it costs when someone else tries to own what he means.
4. **The Mirror** (Ch. 15–19) — one relationship of real mutuality, with a peer who carries her own unwanted label, written with contradiction and self-interest — the book's emotional center outside the family plot.
5. **Reckoning** (Ch. 20–24) — the monster label turns genuinely dangerous; a crisis of mercy vs. violence; an optional return home that is not for reconciliation, and an earned, unsentimental ending.

**Voice pitfalls to guard against past chapter 10:** the closing-aphorism tic calcifying into formula (vary how chapters end); secondary characters staying folkloric-flat in the road's major relationships (at least two need real contradiction); sentimentality creep in mother/mentor figures repeating the "you're not a mistake" beat (say it once, trust the reader after); the "he learned to X, then Y, then Z" triadic list becoming a verbal tic instead of a chosen device; the "monster" label must keep evolving in new concrete instances, not be reasserted by narration.

## 6. Representation & Sensitivity — Read This Before Drafting Further

This premise sits close enough to real disability experience (congenital limb difference, being read as "unfinished," cure-narratives) that dismissing the risk would be a mistake, joke-origin or not. Three specific trope-risks are live:

- **The "curative miracle" trope** — disability resolved by prayer/magic is the oldest, most damaging disability trope there is. The plant-unfolding imagery helps (it reads as transformation, not repair), but the plot structure is still "disabled → non-disabled = the story's central good" unless the prose actively refuses that reading, repeatedly.
- **Disability legible only through a caregiver's suffering** — framing Wendell's early years mainly through what his father *couldn't* do risks making his condition backdrop to an abled person's arc.
- **"Monster" used for gothic frisson rather than indictment** — if cruelty exists to generate atmosphere rather than expose the people wielding the word, the book reproduces the freak-show gaze instead of examining it.

**What's already protecting the book, and must stay load-bearing:** Chapter 1's line — *"Wendell didn't think of himself as unfinished. That was a thing that came from outside him, the way weather comes from outside a house."* — is the single most important sentence in the draft. It needs to function as a recurring refrain the narration returns to at exactly the moments the plot pressures the reader toward pity or horror (the transformation, the father's exit, every road-section cruelty) — not a one-off disclaimer that the plot then quietly overrides.

**On the father:** "I did the head. I can't do the boy" is a genuinely strong, adult theme about conditional love and caregiver-identity collapse — worth telling. The trap is structural: because his devastation follows the cure, plot causality will silently argue "autonomy destroyed the family" unless guarded against. Three concrete guards, already reflected in the outline: (a) seed that his six years of "learning to sit in a room with a head" was already conditional, performative love, not real love — so his collapse reveals a pre-existing rot, not a cure-triggered one; (b) let the mother's bitter dare carry real teeth, partly indicting him, so sympathy is calibrated before he leaves; (c) never give his logic the narrative's last word as a co-equal tragic truth to Wendell's.

**On the carnival/"monster" section:** don't avoid it, don't play it straight. Use it only with Wendell having real agency inside it — choosing to enter the spectacle economy, seeing through the barkers, confronting a showman who tries to own his story — so the scene indicts how "monster" gets manufactured and sold, rather than parading him for the reader's benefit too. Never resolve his arc through proving his worth/humanity to abled onlookers (inspiration-porn with extra steps).

**Sensitivity read: not optional, given the subject matter.** Bring in a limb-different or congenital-disability beta reader / sensitivity consultant at full-manuscript stage (once the road section, ending, and town arc are all on the page), and again before final copyedit, since word-level choices ("monster," "unfinished," "fixed") matter as much as plot architecture.

## 7. Illustration Strategy — Summary

Full detail in `illustrations/00-style-bible.md`. Headline decisions:

- **Style: woodcut/linocut Americana** (bold black linework, limited palette — bone-white, lamp-black, one rust-red or indigo accent), close second Gorey-esque pen-and-ink for close emotional beats. This solves the "how do you *show* a torso unfolding from a neck" problem by rendering it as symbol (a stalk, a root-form) rather than anatomy — literalizing the book's own "plant unfolding" language instead of fighting it with photoreal body-horror.
- **Tooling: ChatGPT's built-in image generator, currently GPT Image 2**, is what you'd actually be using — OpenAI retired the DALL·E brand in March 2025. **"Nano Banana" / "Nano Banana Pro" is Google's Gemini image model, a separate product from a separate company — ChatGPT does not use it.** Stick to ChatGPT alone for the bulk of the book (the consistency workflow depends on staying in one tool); consider Nano Banana Pro only as a supplementary tool for one or two high-stakes assets (cover, a large print spread) where its native 4K/grounded-detail edge matters.
- **Consistency workflow:** write a ~200-word "style bible" paragraph and prefix it verbatim on every prompt (never paraphrase fresh); generate a character sheet first and treat it as the visual anchor; condition every new image on the character sheet plus the most recent accepted image; stay in one long conversation thread and use in-context editing turns rather than fresh regenerations.
- **Budget expectation:** 5–10 generations per finished illustration once your style bible and character sheet are locked (higher, 10–15, while still dialing those in). For 24 chapters: roughly 150–250 total generations, done in batches of 4–5 chapters per sitting.
