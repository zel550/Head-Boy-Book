# WENDELL ROLLS — Full Outline

24 chapters, five books, ~45,000–55,000 words target. One illustration per chapter (see `illustrations/00-style-bible.md` for prompts keyed to these same chapter numbers).

Named characters introduced across the outline (new, beyond the existing Wendell/mother/father, who stay unnamed by design — see `00-project-plan.md` §5 on why that's a deliberate stylistic choice worth preserving):

- **Pete Munro** — an old rail-riding drifter, Wendell's first road mentor.
- **Silas Boyd** — a tenant farmer who nearly shoots Wendell the first night out, then doesn't.
- **Vesta Coombs** — a widow who feeds Wendell without asking questions, after the mob chapter.
- **Reverend Tobias Crane** — a traveling revival showman/con man, the Book Three antagonist.
- **Ruth Calloway** — a girl who lost a leg in a mill accident, Wendell's Book Four counterpart and the book's emotional center outside the family plot.

## BOOK TWO — APPRENTICESHIP IN BEING SEEN (Ch. 4–9)

Establish, in concrete new instances each time (not narrated as a theme), that the "monster"/"strange boy" reputation does two different things: it protects him from casual cruelty, and it isolates him from real belonging. End the book with Wendell deciding to stop being *found* by rumor and start *using* it.

**Ch. 4 — The Barn.** First night on the road. Wendell sleeps in Silas Boyd's barn; Boyd finds him at gunpoint, certain he's dealing with a prowler or something worse once he sees Wendell's face in the lantern light and can't place what he's looking at. He doesn't shoot. He also doesn't help — gives him one meal, tells him to be gone by light. First lesson: people's fear of the unknown can cut either way, and it's a coin flip, not a rule.

**Ch. 5 — Munro.** Wendell falls in with Pete Munro, an old rail-rider, at a water tower outside the next town. Munro teaches him the practical mechanics of the road (riding, begging, reading a town's temperature) and names the thing plainly: "A boy nobody can place is a boy nobody wants trouble with. Use that before it uses you." First time anyone's framed the "monster" reputation as a tool rather than a wound.

**Ch. 6 — The Missing Collection Plate.** In a town called (something plain and real, e.g. Loman's Fork), Wendell is blamed for a theft he didn't commit. The accusation dissolves not because he's cleared but because the accusers get spooked by their own rumor and don't want him in a room, in a jail, in their custody at all — they'd rather he leave than stay and be proven innocent in front of them. Monster label *protects* him, and it costs him: no one ever says he didn't do it.

**Ch. 7 — Dry Season.** A drought, a dead calf, a sick child — small-town superstition needs a cause, and the strange boy passing through fits the shape of one. This escalates to a near-mob at night; Wendell runs, is hurt (cut, or a fall), loses his uncle's knife in the scramble. Monster label turns genuinely dangerous here for the first time — this is the chapter that earns the stakes for the rest of the book.

**Ch. 8 — Vesta Coombs.** Hurt and half-starved, Wendell is taken in for a few days by Vesta Coombs, a widow on the edge of that same county, herself half-shunned by her neighbors for reasons of her own (not disclosed fully — she has her own story, doesn't owe him or the reader access to it). She feeds him, tends the cut, asks nothing, expects nothing, and sends him on with dry socks and a little money. The chapter's function: prove that ordinary decency exists and doesn't require explanation — a deliberate contrast to both the mob and to any "inspirational" reading of Wendell earning kindness through suffering.

**Ch. 9 — The Medicine Show.** Passing through a bigger town, Wendell watches a traveling revival/medicine show from the edge of the crowd — a preacher selling miracle water, working a crowd's hunger for wonder the same way rumor worked against Wendell in Ch. 6 and 7. He recognizes the mechanism. Ends Book Two on a hard turn: rather than keep being defined by what towns decide he is, Wendell approaches the showman afterward, on his own initiative, with a proposition. Close on Reverend Tobias Crane's first line to him — something that promises money and danger in the same breath.

## BOOK THREE — THE EXHIBITION (Ch. 10–14)

Use the carnival/revival set piece deliberately, with Wendell holding real agency throughout — the point is to indict the machinery that manufactures and sells "monster," not to restage it for the reader.

**Ch. 10 — Terms.** Crane and Wendell negotiate. Crane wants a "living testament" act — Wendell tells (an edited, sanded-down, crowd-friendly version of) his own story from a stage. Wendell, clear-eyed, takes the deal for money and the protection of being *inside* the spectacle rather than ambushed by it. Establish Crane as competent, charming, and genuinely dangerous — never a cartoon.

**Ch. 11 — The Living Testament.** First performance. The crowd's reaction (some genuine, some performative, some hostile-under-a-veneer-of-awe) mirrors the whole county's arc from Ch. 2 in miniature, now happening nightly, on command. Wendell is good at it — better than he expected — and that's its own quiet horror.

**Ch. 12 — What It Buys.** Money, food, a kind of safety Wendell hasn't had since before the church. Also: the cost of complicity — Crane starts editing the story further, wants specific lies added (a claim that Crane himself prayed alongside Wendell, e.g., or that the miracle can be repeated with the right donation). Wendell goes along, once, and hates himself for how easy it was.

**Ch. 13 — The Ask.** Crane pushes for something Wendell won't do — staging a fake nightly "regrowth" trick with a hidden second performer, or having Wendell publicly deny his own mother's role in the story to make it play better, or similar — a request that would fully hand over authorship of what Wendell's life means. This is the moral crisis chapter.

**Ch. 14 — Walking Off the Stage.** Wendell refuses, publicly, mid-show if the scene calls for it — exposing Crane's methods to the very crowd Crane built, or simply walking without giving Crane the satisfaction of a scene. Either way, Book Three's climax: Wendell takes back authorship of his own story for the first time since the church. He leaves with less money than he came for and more of himself than he's had since Ch. 3.

## BOOK FOUR — THE MIRROR (Ch. 15–19)

One relationship of real mutuality — the book's emotional center outside the family plot. Ruth Calloway must have contradiction and self-interest, not just symbolic function; this is not a healing-through-romance-alone arc, it's the first time Wendell is known as a whole person by someone who isn't his mother.

**Ch. 15 — Ruth Calloway.** Wendell meets Ruth, who lost a leg in a mill accident two years back and has been managing her own version of a town's altered gaze ever since — pity where he gets fear, but the same basic mechanism of being seen as a case instead of a person. She's sharper than him, funnier, and has no patience for his self-seriousness. Their first exchange should undercut any "fellow-sufferers" sentimentality immediately — she's not moved by his story, she's amused by his manner.

**Ch. 16 — What She Knows That He Doesn't.** Ruth has built an actual life in the after — work, a room she rents, a plan — where Wendell has only ever had flight. She teaches him something practical (a trade, a route, a way to stay somewhere) as much as anything emotional. Keep this reciprocal: he offers her something too (his outsider's read on people, useful to her in some concrete way).

**Ch. 17 — The First Real Fight.** Introduce her self-interest/contradiction concretely: she wants something from the arrangement that isn't just about him (money, cover, an exit from her own situation), and when Wendell realizes it, there's real friction — not a betrayal, a recalibration of what they are to each other. This is the chapter that proves she's a person and not a mirror.

**Ch. 18 — Staying.** Resolution of Ch. 17 into something more honest than before — Wendell, for the first time in the book, considers *staying* somewhere, being known in one place rather than moving on. This is the temptation the ending has to reckon with.

**Ch. 19 — The Knock.** Something from Wendell's past catches up here — a rumor of Crane looking for him (owed money, a grudge, or simply wanting his act back), or word from the old county. Book Four ends on the threat that will drive Book Five, and on Wendell's choice to face it rather than run, partly because Ruth is watching him decide.

## BOOK FIVE — RECKONING (Ch. 20–24)

**Ch. 20 — What Followed Him.** The danger from Ch. 19 arrives in the flesh — Crane, or people acting for him, or the remnant of the Ch. 7 mob's county grown into something organized (a self-appointed "committee" convinced Wendell is owed a reckoning). Real, physical stakes, not atmospheric dread.

**Ch. 21 — Mercy or the Other Thing.** The crisis chapter: Wendell is put in a position where violence is available to him and would even be justified — and has to choose. This is the test of who six years as a head and six years as a hunted boy have actually made him. Avoid a clean pacifist triumph *and* avoid nihilistic violence-begets-violence — the choice should cost him something either way.

**Ch. 22 — The Cost Paid.** Immediate aftermath of Ch. 21 — what the choice actually costs (Ruth hurt, or Wendell himself, or something/someone else). No easy escape from consequence.

**Ch. 23 — The House Again.** Optional, earned return to the childhood house — not for reconciliation. Wendell finds his father unchanged or diminished (older, smaller, sick, or simply still exactly the man who said "I can't do the boy," with nothing new to offer). No apology arc. At most, one line of honesty from the father ("I still can't"). The scene's real content is internal: Wendell stops waiting to be received by him. His mother, if she's still there, gets the real emotional reunion the father doesn't.

**Ch. 24 — Rolling.** Closing chapter and bookend. Wendell on the road again, moving under his own authorship now rather than in flight — ideally with Ruth, or at least toward her, but the ending shouldn't hinge on romance to complete him. Close on an image that deliberately rhymes with the opening (the boy who once could only roll now choosing his own direction on two legs) without spelling the rhyme out in narration. Final thesis, dramatized rather than stated: he was never the mistake; he's the one who got to decide what he became.
